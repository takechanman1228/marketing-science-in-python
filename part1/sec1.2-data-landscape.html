1.2 Understanding Marketing Data

Marketing data rarely lives in a single table. It comes from media platforms, websites and apps, transactions, CRM systems, surveys, product and pricing systems, and external sources. Each captures a different part of customer behavior, at a different level of detail and with different blind spots.

This section uses the marketing funnel as a map of what can be measured, then looks at the datasets and business environments behind those measurements. We will finish with three characteristics that make marketing data especially difficult to interpret.

The Marketing Funnel

Customers rarely buy on first exposure. They move (sometimes over months, sometimes minutes) from “not knowing the brand exists” to “buying” to “buying again.” The most common model of this journey is the funnel:

Awareness → Consideration → Conversion → Retention

At the top of the funnel, we often rely on indirect or aggregate signals such as reach, impressions, and brand awareness. Further down the funnel, behavior becomes easier to observe directly through visits, purchases, repeat purchases, and customer-level transactions.

KPIs across the Funnel

Each stage of the funnel comes with a different set of metrics.

Funnel Stage Typical KPIs What They Measure
Awareness Reach, Impressions, Aided / Unaided Brand Awareness, Share of Voice Whether people know you exist
Consideration CTR (click-through rate), Site Visits, Time on Site, Search Volume, Branded Search Lift Whether people are evaluating you
Conversion CVR (conversion rate), CAC (customer acquisition cost), AOV (average order value), ROAS (return on ad spend) Whether people buy, and how efficiently
Retention Repeat Rate, Churn Rate, Retention Curve, CLV / LTV (customer lifetime value), NPS (net promoter score) Whether people come back

The goal is not to optimize each stage of the funnel independently. Improving one metric can make another look worse. For example, expanding reach or traffic often brings in more marginal prospects, which can increase search volume or site visits while lowering conversion rate. Similarly, reducing CAC by shifting toward cheaper channels or broader audiences may bring in customers who are less likely to return, lowering repeat rate or CLV. What matters is whether the funnel as a whole is creating more revenue and profit.

Marketing Data across Industries

The same marketing question can look very different depending on the business.

What data is available, and therefore which methods are practical, varies substantially across industries.

Industry Typical Data Relevant Methods in This Book
CPG Retail POS; panel and survey data; media and trade spend MMM; incrementality experiments; sales forecasting; price elasticity; assortment optimization; quasi-experiments
Retail (Offline) POS and loyalty data; inventory Customer segmentation; CLV; price elasticity; assortment optimization; quasi-experiments; sales forecasting
E-commerce Clickstream; transactions; CRM; media data A/B testing; uplift modeling; segmentation and CLV; sales forecasting
Consumer App Acquisition data; in-app behavior; subscriptions or purchases A/B testing; uplift modeling; CLV; causal inference
SaaS Product usage; CRM; billing; support data A/B testing; CLV; pricing; sales forecasting

These differences matter because they change how an analysis can be designed.

An e-commerce or app business can often randomize a message or product experience at the user level and observe the outcome directly. In offline retail, a store-level A/B test is harder because stores differ in traffic, customer mix, assortment, geography, and local conditions, making clean treatment-control comparisons more difficult.

A CPG manufacturer may face another constraint: much of the purchase data is generated by retailers rather than by the brand itself, so direct customer-level observation can be limited.

Why Marketing Data Is Hard

So far, the datasets may look like familiar data science inputs: tables to collect, join, clean, and model.

The harder problem is how those data were generated.

Three characteristics appear repeatedly in marketing work.

Characteristic 1: Most Is Hidden

In marketing, we usually see only the tip of the iceberg. We observe clicks, impressions, sessions, and sales, but many important drivers stay hidden: customer needs, brand perception, competitor actions, seasonality, pricing context, and many other influences.

This means the data is always an incomplete view of what caused the outcome. Teams naturally focus on what is easy to measure, even when the most important drivers sit outside the dataset. Good analysis therefore starts with humility about what the data can and cannot reveal.

Characteristic 2: Data Is Messy

Marketing data is not created for a controlled research study. It is created by real business operations.

Tracking breaks. Events go missing. Metrics are defined differently across platforms. Units and levels of aggregation do not line up. APIs, attribution rules, and user interfaces change over time.

As a result, even basic questions often require judgment before they require modeling. Clean analysis depends not only on statistical skill, but also on careful data validation and a clear understanding of how the data was produced.

Characteristic 3: Everything Happens Together

In real marketing systems, multiple touchpoints act at once. Search, TV, social, email, promotions, pricing, and distribution often move together rather than independently.

That makes cause and effect harder to separate. If sales increase, the data alone may not tell us which activity deserves the credit. In modeling terms, the signals are entangled; in business terms, several actions changed at the same time.

These are not reasons to avoid analysis. They are reminders to be cautious, skeptical, and precise about what the data can support.
