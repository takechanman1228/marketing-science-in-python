4.1 Customer Segmentation

A segmentation project should produce something the team can put into a campaign. The deliverable is a segment_id that connects directly to your CRM and ad platforms, plus a concise brief for each segment: who they are, why they matter, and what actions your team should take.

Segmentation is not one method. Deciles, RFM, K-Means, and embedding-based clustering each answer a different question. The question comes from the decision the segments have to support: who gets the retention budget, which message goes to whom, which product story to tell.

Choose the Segmentation by the Question

Match the decision to a row.

These are three lenses rather than three stages of one pipeline. A later row does not refine an earlier row’s segments; it answers a different question, sometimes on different data. Start from the simplest row that would change the decision, and move down only when two customers the current lens treats alike would get different treatment. The chapter builds the first two rows on one grocery retailer, the Dunnhumby “The Complete Journey” dataset (2,500 households over two years), and the third on a gift retailer whose product text suits it.

Behavior First, Profiles After

Behavioral variables describe what customers do: recency, frequency, spend, categories purchased, product breadth, returns, and discount dependence. Profile variables describe who they are or where to reach them: age, income, household composition, geography, loyalty tier, channel preference. Build the segments on behavior: labels such as “a man in his 30s” or “a high earner” predict the next purchase far less well than what someone actually bought. Bring the profile variables in afterward, to describe each segment and to choose where and how to reach it.

Value Tiers: Deciles and RFM

The fastest segmentation is a ranking. Decile analysis splits customers into ten equal groups by revenue, and the grocery data shows the usual concentration: the top two deciles hold 53% of revenue.

A decile says where the value sits, which is what you need when the decision is how much attention each customer gets. It says nothing about why customers differ or which message will work.

RFM adds engagement to value, in the terms direct-marketing teams already use: lapsed high-spenders, new frequent buyers.

Score each variable into quintiles and combine them, and a high spender who bought yesterday separates from one silent for a year. The combined score has up to 125 cells, so collapse the cells that would get the same treatment. PyMC-Marketing, the library the CLV chapter uses, does the scoring in one call with rfm_segments().

Deciles and RFM measure how much and how recently customers buy, not how. Two customers with the same RFM score can behave very differently: one buys full-price items across many categories, the other only discounted items in a single category.

Behavioral Segments with K-Means

To separate those two customers, describe how they shop. K-Means is a classic clustering algorithm that still works well for this. Start with 6 to 10 behavioral variables: beyond recency, frequency, and spend, add average order value, basket size, category breadth, discount share, and return rate. Normalize them with StandardScaler so spend does not dominate.

Log-transform the skewed variables before scaling. Spend, frequency, recency, and basket size are heavily right-skewed, and StandardScaler only centers and rescales, so the long tails survive. Fed the raw features, K-Means uses its first splits to separate big spenders from small ones and mostly reproduces the value tiers. Applying log1p to the skewed features (a simple rule: transform anything with skew above about 1) compresses the tails, so the clusters separate customers by purchase pattern instead of spend level. On the grocery data this is the difference between K-Means collapsing to three value tiers and splitting the base into five balanced behavioral groups.

Choosing K uses both the diagnostics and the team’s judgment. The elbow method narrows the range but rarely pins the number, and on customer data its sharpest bend often sits at K=3, which just recovers the value tiers that deciles and RFM already capture. Push into the practical range of 4 to 8 and let actionability decide: keep the K whose segments your marketing team would treat differently.

On the grocery data, with K=5 the segments differ in purchase pattern as well as in spending. Figure 3 is the profile behind that fit: one row per segment, one column per behavioral feature, colored by the segment mean against the population.

When Product Meaning Matters

Numeric features describe how customers shop, not what they buy. When the decision is about category marketing, cross-sell, or creative, the signal is in the products themselves, so you need product text. This section is therefore a separate route rather than the next step after K-Means, and it runs on different data. The UCI Online Retail II dataset, about a million transactions from a UK gift retailer, has the free-text product descriptions an embedding model can read.

Treat each customer’s purchase history as text: concatenate their purchased product descriptions into a single string (the customer document), then use an embedding model to turn that string into a dense vector. In this embedding space, birthday candles and party plates have a short distance between them and an antique teapot is far from both, so clustering the vectors groups customers by what they buy rather than by how much they spend. For example:

red retrospot cake stand, party bunting, paper cups, birthday candles, cake cases

Numeric features place this customer in a frequent, high-spend tier. The embedding places them near other buyers of party supplies. Building the document is a group-and-concatenate over the transaction log:

# Keep each customer's top-N products by revenue, then join the descriptions
prod_rev = (
    df.groupby(["CustomerID", "Description"])["Revenue"].sum()
    .reset_index()
    .sort_values(["CustomerID", "Revenue"], ascending=[True, False])
)
top_products = prod_rev.groupby("CustomerID").head(TOP_N)

customer_docs = (
    top_products.groupby("CustomerID")["Description"]
    .apply(lambda d: ", ".join(d))
    .reset_index(name="doc")
)

Keep each customer’s top products rather than the full history, filter generic items such as “Postage” that appear in every segment, and cap the weight of any single expensive purchase. These choices matter more than the choice of embedding model.

BERTopic runs the rest of the pipeline in one call: a local sentence-embedding model turns each document into a vector, and BERTopic clusters the vectors and extracts each cluster’s distinctive keywords.

from bertopic import BERTopic
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer

embedding_model = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")   # local, lightweight
embeddings = embedding_model.encode(customer_docs["doc"].tolist())

topic_model = BERTopic(embedding_model=embedding_model)
topics, probs = topic_model.fit_transform(
    customer_docs["doc"].tolist(), embeddings=embeddings
)

Figure 5 shows the seven topics BERTopic found, and the keywords name the niches directly: party candles and lanterns, chocolate-spot cakeware, feltcraft kits, airline bags.

What the fingerprints do not show is how the base is distributed across them, and on this catalog that is the finding. The broad topic (decoration, candles, wreaths) holds 92% of customers once the ones HDBSCAN left unassigned are folded back in; the six niches together hold about 8%. The notebook splits the broad topic with K-Means into six sub-segments, but a re-fit on a later period does not reproduce them: the adjusted Rand index is near zero, against about 0.14 for an RFM K-Means on the same customers. Nine of the twelve names an LLM assigned are variations on the same home-decor theme. Read this segmentation as discovery, a description of what the catalog sells to whom, and keep the CRM segment_id on the behavioral segmentation.

The keywords are also the guard against LLM-assisted naming. Feed any cluster, even a random one, into an LLM and ask for a name and persona, and you will get a polished, convincing description. Two safeguards:

  • Read the top keywords before the LLM’s description. If you cannot see a clear theme yourself, the name is not supported by the data.
  • Test the names with the marketing team. If two segments would receive the same campaign, separating them adds little value for that decision.

Is Your Segmentation Any Good?

Check three things, in this order: actionability, stability, and size.

Actionability matters most. Show the segment profiles to your marketing team and, for each pair of segments, ask whether they would use a different message, channel, offer, or frequency. If they would not, the segmentation is not actionable, however good its clustering metrics are. The usual cause is a segment that is average on everything; merge it with a neighbor or revisit your variable selection.

The second check is stability. Re-run the segmentation on two non-overlapping periods and check that customers mostly land in the same segments; the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) puts a number on this. On the grocery data the notebook checks the value ranking rather than the clusters: it places households in revenue quintiles in each year and follows where they move.

The top and bottom quintiles mostly stay put (70% and 61%), while the middle three hold only about 40%, so a campaign built on the middle of the base needs the most slack. That says how stable the value ranking is, not how stable the five segments are. Re-fitting the K-Means on a second period is the check to run before the segment_id drives a campaign.

The third check is size. Each segment should be big enough to act on, but there is no universal floor. For broad targeting that often means around 10 percent of the base; a genuinely high-value niche can be a deliberate exception.

From Segments to a segment_id

The output that leaves the project is one row per customer. The companion notebook writes 2,500 rows to segment_assignments.csv, three of them below, and the CLV chapter joins this file to put a value on each segment.

Keep each segment’s brief next to the file and version the file: a membership that silently changes between campaigns is worse than none. Segments describe differences between customers; they do not show that a message works for one of them. Once a treatment is chosen, test it: the methods in A/B Test Design apply to segment campaigns as much as to any other.

Key Takeaways

  • Choose the segmentation by the decision it serves. Deciles and RFM rank value, K-Means finds behavioral groups, and embeddings capture what customers buy; a later method answers a different question and does not refine the earlier one’s segments.
  • Build segments from behavior and validate for actionability first, then stability and size. If two segments would get the same campaign, merge them.
  • An LLM will produce a convincing name for any cluster, including a random one, so read the top keywords yourself before you trust the label.
