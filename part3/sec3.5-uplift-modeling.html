3.5 Uplift Modeling for Targeting

In §3.4 we estimated each customer’s treatment effect, the CATE. That was measurement. This chapter is about action: turning those per-customer scores into a decision about whom to target.

Most marketing teams target with a response model: predict who is most likely to buy, then aim the budget at the top of that list. It feels right, but it quietly wastes money. Many of the customers most likely to buy would have bought anyway, so the spend on them buys nothing. The customers who matter are the ones whose behavior actually changes when you contact them, and they are not the same people. That is what an uplift model ranks:

  • A response model predicts: \(P(\text{purchase})\)
  • An uplift model predicts: \(P(\text{purchase} \mid \text{treated}) - P(\text{purchase} \mid \text{not treated})\)

The second quantity is exactly the CATE from §3.4. So we already have an uplift score; this chapter builds the workflow that turns it into a targeting policy, and checks on held-out data that the ranking is worth acting on.

The Four Customer Types

It helps to picture four kinds of customer, defined by how they respond to being contacted.

  • Persuadables: buy only if contacted. These are the ideal targets; the e-mail changes their behavior, and your whole return comes from them.
  • Sure Things: buy either way. Contacting them spends budget for no incremental sale.
  • Lost Causes: never buy. Contact is wasted.
  • Sleeping Dogs: would buy on their own, but contact pushes them away. Reaching them actively costs you.

A response model, chasing likelihood to buy, fills its list with Sure Things. An uplift model, chasing the treatment effect, aims for Persuadables and steers clear of Sleeping Dogs. That difference is the whole point.

Scoring Customers by Uplift

The uplift score is the CATE from §3.4. Any of the meta-learners there produces one. In this run the S-Learner gives the cleanest recovery, so we carry its CATE forward as the uplift score; the notebook’s recovery table has the full comparison. The library is the same EconML we used in §3.4, so there is nothing new to install:

from econml.metalearners import SLearner
from lightgbm import LGBMRegressor

uplift = SLearner(overall_model=LGBMRegressor())
uplift.fit(Y=conversion_train, T=treatment_train, X=X_train)
uplift_score = uplift.effect(X_eval)   # one CATE / uplift score per customer

We test the workflow on the same semi-synthetic MineThatData example as §3.4: the real customer features and the real randomized assignment to a Men’s-merchandise e-mail or no e-mail, from Kevin Hillstrom’s e-mail experiment (42,613 customers, split 50/50), with outcomes simulated from a treatment effect we define. The point of simulating is honesty, not convenience. Because we know each customer’s true effect, we can grade the model against the truth at every step, something a noisy real dataset never lets you do. The average effect we built in is about 11 percentage points, and it is larger for men’s buyers, recently active customers, and multichannel shoppers. Treat this as a teaching bed, not a promise that uplift always works; the guardrails at the end say what changes in the wild.

Does the Ranking Beat Random?

A targeting score is only useful if ranking by it concentrates the incremental conversions near the top. The standard check is the uplift curve: rank customers best-first, then plot the cumulative incremental conversions you capture as you target deeper. A useful ranking bends above the straight diagonal you would get by contacting customers in random order.

The curve climbs steeply over the first few deciles and stays well above the random line: most of the incremental conversions are captured early, which is the concentration that makes targeting pay. The Qini curve tells the same story on a treatment-normalized scale, and is more robust when the treated share drifts across the ranking. Both summary areas are positive (AUUC 0.04, Qini 0.07), which is the formal way of saying the ranking beats random. These are relative scores: positive means better than random, and they are comparable only on the same data, not against an absolute scale.

Where the Uplift Lives

To make the ranking concrete, cut the held-out customers into deciles by uplift score and read the uplift inside each. Because the data is semi-synthetic, we can show three columns side by side: the model’s predicted uplift, the observed uplift on the randomized holdout (the treated-minus-control conversion rate inside the decile), and the true uplift.

The top decile carries about 17 percentage points of observed uplift against an 11-point average, and the observed and true columns track the predicted one down the ranking. The predicted column is monotone by construction; the observed column is noisier, since each decile holds only about 1,300 customers, so a few middle deciles wobble out of order. The lesson is to read the top of the ranking, not individual rows in the tail.

Whom to Target

A score becomes a decision only once you attach economics. Say each incremental conversion is worth $30 in margin and contacting a customer costs $3. Then a customer is worth treating only when their uplift clears a 10% break-even, and the expected net value of treating a decile is its uplift times the conversion value, minus the contact cost.

Under these (illustrative) numbers, the top five deciles clear positive expected net value and the bottom five do not, so the policy is simple: contact the top half of the list and leave the rest alone. The constants are not the point; the rule is. Convert uplift into expected value, subtract the cost of contact, and target only where the result is positive. That is your customer list. The predicted and observed columns mostly agree on where to draw the line; where they diverge near the cutoff is exactly where a fresh holdout earns its keep before you commit budget.

Guardrails

A few cautions carry over to any real targeting program.

  • Net the cost before deciding. Target where incremental value clears the cost of contact, not wherever the score is merely positive. The net-value rule, not the raw score, sets the budget.
  • Watch for fatigue. Even high-uplift customers disengage if contacted too often. Cap frequency and watch whether uplift decays for recently contacted customers.
  • Mind the Sleeping Dogs. Real campaigns can have negative-uplift customers whom contact pushes away; the net-value rule already steers you out of the bottom deciles where they hide.
  • Validate on a fresh experiment. We could grade against the truth here only because the data is semi-synthetic. In the wild the true effect is invisible, so confirm a policy’s incremental value on a new randomized holdout before trusting it, and remember that an uplift model is not guaranteed to beat a simple response model on every dataset.

That wraps up Part 3. We began with a colleague’s claim that a coupon doubled the purchase rate and found it was just selection bias. From there we built a toolkit to answer the real question: not whether a campaign works on average, but who it works on, and whether acting on that is worth it. We covered A/B testing, quasi-experiments, meta-learners, and uplift modeling. The throughline is a single sentence: estimate who responds incrementally, then decide whom to target.
