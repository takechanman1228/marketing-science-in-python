3.2 A/B Test Design

Your team just finished a two-week A/B test on the new checkout page. Everyone is eager to see the results. Then the numbers come in:

Variant Users Conversions CVR
Control 5,000 115 2.30%
Treatment 5,000 127 2.54%

Treatment looks a little better, a 0.24-point lift. But a two-proportion z-test puts the p-value at 0.43:

import numpy as np
from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportions_ztest

conversions = np.array([127, 115])   # treatment, control
users = np.array([5000, 5000])
stat, p_value = proportions_ztest(conversions, users, alternative="two-sided")
print(f"p-value: {p_value:.2f}")     # 0.43

So the team says, “Not significant. The redesign doesn’t work. Let’s move on.” That conclusion isn’t backed up by the data. A p-value of 0.43 doesn’t mean there’s no effect. It means the test couldn’t separate a small effect from random noise. The treatment might really be better; the data just can’t say. The gap between “no effect” and “we can’t tell” comes down to whether the test had enough power.

Statistical Power

Statistical power is the chance your test will detect an effect of a specific size if that effect truly exists. Most teams aim for 80% power, which still leaves a 20% chance of missing an effect that large.

Imagine repeating the same A/B test many times. If the redesign has no real effect, the estimated lift will bounce around zero. If the redesign truly improves conversion, the estimated lift will bounce around the real lift. Power is the chance that the test lands far enough from zero for us to detect that effect.

For a low-conversion event like a purchase (say a 2% conversion rate), detecting a 0.5-point lift takes many thousands of users per group, and most teams run with far fewer. This is the part most teams underestimate: detecting small lifts usually requires far more traffic than intuition suggests.

Calculating Sample Size

So how many users do you actually need? Sizing a test takes four inputs: the baseline conversion rate, the minimum lift worth detecting (the MDE), the false-positive rate you’ll tolerate (\(\alpha\), usually 0.05), and the power you want (\(1 - \beta\), usually 0.80).

The key intuition is:

\[n \propto \frac{1}{\text{MDE}^2}\]

If you want to detect half the lift, you need roughly four times as many users. In Python:

from statsmodels.stats.power import NormalIndPower
from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportion_effectsize

baseline_cvr = 0.02
target_cvr = 0.025
effect_size = abs(proportion_effectsize(target_cvr, baseline_cvr))

analysis = NormalIndPower()
required_n = analysis.solve_power(
    effect_size=effect_size,
    alpha=0.05,
    power=0.80,
    ratio=1,
    alternative="two-sided",
)
print(f"Required sample size per group: {required_n:.0f}")  # ~13,800

Detecting a lift from a 2% baseline to 2.5% needs about 13,800 users per group. In practice teams round up and pad for tracking loss and excluded users, so they would plan for roughly 15,000.

The curve makes the main lesson visible: sample size does not grow linearly. Moving from a 1.0 percentage point lift to a 0.5 percentage point lift roughly quadruples the required sample. This is why small improvements on low-conversion metrics are expensive to measure.

Decide Before You Launch

Before launching the test, write down four decisions:

  1. Primary metric: which metric decides the test?
  2. MDE: what lift is large enough to matter?
  3. Sample size and duration: how much data will you collect before judging the result?
  4. Guardrail metrics: what should not get worse (revenue per user, page-load time, support tickets)?

Writing these down up front guards against two habits that quietly inflate false positives: peeking (stopping the test the moment it looks significant) and metric fishing (reporting whichever of many metrics happened to move).

CUPED: Getting More Precision from the Same Sample

When the required sample is out of reach, you can’t always add more users, stores, or markets. CUPED (short for Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data) can sometimes give you more precision from the same data by using information from before the experiment. Microsoft’s experimentation team introduced it in 2013 to make online experiments more sensitive without collecting more data (Deng et al. 2013), and it has since become a standard variance-reduction tool in large-scale experimentation.

The idea is simple. If a customer was already a high spender before the experiment, they will probably keep spending during it too, treatment or not. CUPED subtracts that predictable part of the outcome, so the comparison focuses on what actually changed during the test.

In the figure, Customer A spent $100 before the campaign and $110 during it. Given that history you’d expect about $102, so the lift above expectation is just $8, not the full $110. Stripping out each customer’s predictable spend tightens the spread of outcomes without changing the average treatment effect, so the effect becomes easier to detect.

The same idea helps in store-level or market-level experiments. Suppose a retailer wants to test an in-store promotion. Randomizing hundreds of stores into treatment and control may be operationally unrealistic, and every store is different: some are larger, some have different customer bases, some are hit by local events. CUPED can use each store’s pre-experiment sales to remove part of that predictable store-to-store variation. It doesn’t create more stores or fix a weak design, but when pre-period sales strongly predict sales during the test, it can make a smaller store test more precise.

Formally, let \(Y\) be the outcome and \(X\) a pre-experiment covariate (e.g., purchases in the prior 30 days). The adjusted outcome is:

\[Y_{\text{CUPED}} = Y - \theta \cdot (X - \bar{X})\]

where \(\theta = \text{Cov}(Y, X) / \text{Var}(X)\). The variance of the adjusted outcome reduces to \(\text{Var}(Y_{\text{CUPED}}) = \text{Var}(Y)(1 - \rho^2)\), where \(\rho\) is the Pearson correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\). If \(\rho = 0.7\), CUPED removes about half the variance, as much precision as doubling the sample would buy. Netflix later published case studies reporting similar variance reductions across its large-scale online experiments (Xie and Aurisset 2016). The most common covariate is the same metric measured in the pre-experiment period, which usually gives the strongest correlation.

In code, the adjustment is only a few lines. On a simulated campaign where pre-period spend correlates with in-experiment spend at about 0.75, it cuts the standard error of the lift by about a third:

import numpy as np

# df: one row per customer — pre_spend (prior 30 days), campaign_spend, treatment (0/1)
theta = np.cov(df["campaign_spend"], df["pre_spend"])[0, 1] / df["pre_spend"].var()
df["cuped_spend"] = df["campaign_spend"] - theta * (
    df["pre_spend"] - df["pre_spend"].mean()
)

for metric in ["campaign_spend", "cuped_spend"]:
    t = df.loc[df["treatment"] == 1, metric]
    c = df.loc[df["treatment"] == 0, metric]
    se = np.sqrt(t.var() / len(t) + c.var() / len(c))
    print(f"{metric:15s} lift={t.mean() - c.mean():.2f}  SE={se:.3f}")
# campaign_spend  lift=0.97  SE=0.549
# cuped_spend     lift=1.07  SE=0.364   (33.7% smaller SE — like a much bigger sample)

The point estimate shifts slightly (0.97 → 1.07): CUPED removes the part of the raw gap caused by a chance pre-period imbalance between the groups, so it still targets the same true effect while the interval tightens. The companion notebook simulates df end to end (reporting p-values, which here drop from 0.08 to 0.003) so you can change the correlation and watch the precision gain shrink or grow.

When a Test Is Inconclusive

Return to the checkout test. The right question isn’t “was the p-value below 0.05?” It’s “was the test precise enough to rule out an effect we’d care about?”

Look at the confidence interval. The observed lift was +0.24 points, but the 95% interval runs from −0.36 to +0.84 points, wide enough to include both a small loss and a sizable win. That test is inconclusive: it hasn’t shown the redesign works, but it certainly hasn’t shown it doesn’t. Only when the interval is narrow and excludes any lift worth shipping can you reasonably set a treatment aside.

A non-significant result, in other words, is not automatically a failed treatment. It may simply mean the test was too small, too noisy, or run during an unusual period.

But what if you can’t randomize at all? Maybe the treatment is already live, or there are ethical reasons you can’t run an experiment. That’s when you turn to quasi-experimental methods.
