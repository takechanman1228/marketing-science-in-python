3.3 Quasi Experiments

A/B testing is the gold standard for measuring cause and effect. Randomize who gets the treatment, and the control group tells you what would have happened without it. But you can’t always run one. As Section 3.1 covered, there are plenty of reasons an A/B test isn’t an option.

When you can’t randomize, you use a quasi-experiment. A quasi-experiment estimates cause and effect without random assignment. It works with whatever comparison the business process left behind: a cutoff, a rollout that skipped some units, a launch date. That last part is the catch. A non-random rollout isn’t automatically a usable quasi-experiment. It has to leave a comparison whose assumptions you can defend, and each design in this chapter comes with the assumption you’ll have to defend.

This chapter covers three designs: Regression Discontinuity (RDD), Difference-in-Differences (DiD), and Synthetic Control. Each one needs untreated units to compare against: a control group of stores that didn’t get the display, or a pool of stores to blend into one. The fourth design, CausalImpact, is for when there are no untreated units at all. It gets the next chapter.

Which Design Fits Your Situation?

Four designs is a lot to choose from, and the names don’t tell you which one you need. So let’s put them all on one concrete problem. A retailer has 100 stores. It rolled out a new in-store display and wants to know what the display did to category sales.

The first question is always the same: was the display assigned at random? If it was, the untreated stores are your control group and you’re back in Section 3.2. If it wasn’t, the way it was assigned picks the design.

Every row has the same 100 stores. What changed is how the display was assigned, and that decides both the design and the data you need: the running variable for RDD, a before-and-after panel for DiD, a long pre-period and a pool of untreated stores for Synthetic Control. Fifty stores chosen at random and fifty chosen by the sales team can produce the same spreadsheet, and only the first is an experiment. That’s the rule for the whole chapter. The assignment picks the design. The data then decides whether the design is credible.

That table is one retailer’s five options. Figure 1 turns it into a router for any rollout: two yes-or-no questions, then a choice that depends on how much untreated data you have left.

The four designs aren’t four unrelated tricks, either. One of them, RDD, uses a cutoff and nothing else. The other three form a ladder. DiD borrows the trend of the control stores. Synthetic Control blends control stores into a better comparison when no single one will do. CausalImpact forecasts the comparison when there are no control stores at all. Each rung leans harder on a model. This chapter covers RDD and the first two rungs.

Regression Discontinuity (RDD)

Core idea: right around the cutoff, the assignment is as good as random.

Start with the second row of Table 1. Instead of picking the stores, the sales team wrote a rule: every store above $1M in annual sales gets the new display. The pilot stores are now the biggest stores by construction. Comparing them to the rest would just measure what it’s like to be big.

But look at the stores near the line. A store doing $1.02M and a store doing $0.98M are about the same size, serve similar shoppers, and run similar promotions. Which side of $1M they landed on is close to a coin flip. RDD treats it as one. It compares outcomes just above the cutoff to outcomes just below it, and if category sales jump at $1M, the jump is the effect of the display. The same logic works for customers: if customers with at least $500 of past spend get a coupon, compare the $502 customers to the $498 ones.

When RDD doesn’t work

RDD needs a lot of units near the cutoff, because it uses only those. With 100 stores, maybe ten sit within a few percent of $1M, and that’s too thin to fit a line on each side and see a jump above the noise. With 20,000 customers near $500, it isn’t. That’s why RDD is mostly a customer-level tool in marketing: coupons or points based on past spend, lead-score cutoffs for sales outreach. Figure 2 shows that version.

Two more limits. The effect is local: the jump at $500 says what the coupon did for customers who spend about $500, not for customers who spend $5,000. And the units can’t be able to move themselves across the line. If a rewards program promotes customers to the next tier at $1,000 and customers know it, some will make one extra purchase in December to get over. If a store manager gets a bonus for hitting $1M, stores at $0.97M in November find a way to finish at $1.01M. Either way, the units just above the cutoff are the ones who tried harder, and RDD reads that as the effect of the treatment. A histogram of the running variable with a pile just above the cutoff is the tell. On a threshold like that, RDD can’t be used.

Difference-in-Differences (DiD)

Core idea: compare changes, not levels. The control group’s change over time stands in for what the treated group would have done.

Now the third row of Table 1: the sales team picked 50 stores for the new display, and 50 comparable stores kept the standard shelf layout. You have category sales per store, before and after the rollout, for both groups.

The pilot stores went from $42k to $48k. So the display is worth $6k per store?

Probably not. The comparison stores went from $40k to $43k with no display at all. Something lifted the whole category by about $3k: seasonality, traffic, a competitor’s stockout. A before-and-after comparison on the pilot stores alone books that market growth as display effect.

So compare the two groups after the rollout instead. $48k against $43k is $5k. Is that the effect?

Also no. The pilot stores were already $2k ahead before anything happened, at $42k against $40k. The sales team didn’t pick at random, and a treated-versus-control comparison in the post period books that head start as display effect.

Each naive comparison fails on a different confound. Before-versus-after catches the shared time trend. Treated-versus-control catches the pre-existing gap between the groups. Take the difference of the two differences, and both cancel:

Store group Before After Change
Pilot stores $42k $48k +$6k
Comparison stores $40k $43k +$3k
DiD estimate +$3k

Pilot stores grew by $6k, comparison stores by $3k, and the difference is $3k. You get the same answer the other way around: a $5k gap after minus a $2k gap before. That’s Difference-in-Differences. The shared trend cancels out. So does any fixed difference between the groups. What’s left is the part of the pilot stores’ change that the comparison stores can’t explain.

In code, the estimate is just those two differences:

import pandas as pd

did = pd.DataFrame({
    "group":  ["Pilot stores", "Comparison stores"],
    "before": [42, 40],   # category sales per store, $k
    "after":  [48, 43],
})
did["change"] = did["after"] - did["before"]              # +6 and +3
did_estimate = did.loc[0, "change"] - did.loc[1, "change"]
print(f"DiD estimate: ${did_estimate}k")                  # DiD estimate: $3k

DiD fits most “some units changed, others didn’t” situations in marketing: store display pilots, shelf resets, regional promotion rollouts, pricing changes in selected markets, an app feature launched on one platform first.

The Parallel Trends Assumption

DiD rests on parallel trends (also called common trends): without the treatment, both groups would have followed the same trend. Trend, not level. The groups can start at different sales levels, as the pilot stores do. What matters is that the comparison group’s change after the rollout is a fair stand-in for what the pilot stores would have done on their own.

You can never fully verify that, because you don’t observe the pilot stores without the display. But you can check the period before the rollout.

Plot both groups over time and look at the part before the display. If the two lines move together, DiD is credible. A clean pre-trend is a credibility check, not a proof, but it’s the check everyone will ask for.

When DiD doesn’t work

DiD fails when the sales team picked on momentum. If they chose stores that were already growing faster, the two groups don’t share a trend, and the DiD estimate takes credit for growth that was coming anyway. You’ll see it in the pre-period plot: the lines were already diverging before the display. When that happens, don’t force DiD. Match the groups more carefully, weight the comparison stores, or move to Synthetic Control.

Estimating DiD with a Regression

The two-by-two table is the idea. It isn’t what you’d report, for two reasons. It gives no standard error, so you can’t say whether +$3k is distinguishable from zero. And real data isn’t two periods; it’s 24 months of sales for 100 stores. The regression version handles both. One row per store per month, a fixed effect for each store, a fixed effect for each month, and one interaction term for treated stores in the post period. The store effects absorb every fixed difference between stores. The month effects absorb every trend and shock that hit all stores that month. The interaction term is the DiD estimate.

import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

# df: one row per store-month (store_id, period, treated, post, category_sales)
model = smf.ols(
    "category_sales ~ C(store_id) + C(period) + treated:post",
    data=df,
).fit(cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": df["store_id"]})

coef, se = model.params["treated:post"], model.bse["treated:post"]
print(f"DiD estimate: {coef:.2f} (SE {se:.2f})")   # DiD estimate: 3.02 (SE 0.04)

Clustering the standard error by store handles the repeated measurements for each store. The same regression checks pre-trends: replace treated:post with one treated × month term per month and confirm the pre-rollout terms are close to zero. The companion notebook walks through the full panel and that check.

Synthetic Control

Core idea: when no single control unit matches, build one by mixing several.

DiD worked because 50 control stores gave you a convincing trend to borrow. Now take the fourth row of Table 1: only the New York store got the display. Which of the other 99 stores is the right comparison? They differ in size, category mix, and local competition, and no single store is a convincing stand-in for New York.

Synthetic Control doesn’t pick one. It treats the other 99 stores as a donor pool and builds a “synthetic New York” as a weighted mix of them, choosing the weights so that the blend tracks New York’s sales as closely as possible before the display went in. In the companion notebook the blend comes out as 36% of the Boston store, 28% of Philadelphia, 22% of Chicago, and a few percent spread over four other stores. Once the display is in, the gap between actual New York and its synthetic twin is the estimated effect.

It’s the natural design whenever one unit gets the treatment and many similar units don’t: one store remodeled, one metro with an out-of-home campaign, one SKU with a price change and its sibling SKUs as donors. These geo-level designs come back in Section 6.3 as a robustness check for geo experiments and in Section 6.4 as the calibration anchor for media mix models.

When Synthetic Control doesn’t work

The credibility check is the pre-period fit. If no blend of the 99 stores can reproduce New York’s history before the display, don’t trust the gap after it. The donors also have to be untouched: a display in New York doesn’t move sales in Chicago, but a New York TV campaign might reach New Jersey, and a donor that caught the treatment drags the synthetic twin toward the treated path. And it can’t do one customer. A single customer’s weekly spend is mostly zeros and noise, and no blend of other customers will track it. Customer-level questions need many customers, which is DiD or RDD territory.

When There Is No Control Group

DiD borrowed a trend from control stores. Synthetic Control blended control stores when no single one would do. RDD compared stores just below a line to stores just above it. All three borrow the comparison from units the action didn’t reach, and none of them can help when it reached everyone:

  • The new display went into all 100 stores on the same day.
  • The campaign ran in every region, not in a few.
  • The coupon went to every customer.

There’s no untreated store, region, or customer to compare against. So the comparison has to be forecast: from what the series was doing before the action, and from what other series the action couldn’t touch did after it. That’s the last rung of the ladder, CausalImpact, and it’s the next chapter.

Key Takeaways

  • Start from how the treatment was assigned, not from the estimator. Random assignment gives you an A/B test. Anything else gives you a quasi-experiment, and the assignment picks which one.
  • RDD estimates a local effect for units near the cutoff, and only when there are many of them and none can move themselves across the line. Don’t read the jump at the threshold as an effect for everyone.
  • DiD exists because both naive comparisons fail. Before-versus-after books the market trend; treated-versus-control books the pre-existing gap. The difference of the differences cancels both, but only if the parallel trends assumption holds. Check the pre-period, and treat a clean pre-trend as a credibility check, not proof.
  • Synthetic Control stands or falls on the pre-period fit. If the weighted donor blend can’t reproduce the treated unit’s history, don’t trust the gap that follows.
  • DiD, Synthetic Control, and CausalImpact are a ladder: borrow the control’s trend, blend the controls, forecast the control. Each rung leans harder on a model. When the action reached every store, region, or customer, you’re on the last rung, which is the next chapter.
