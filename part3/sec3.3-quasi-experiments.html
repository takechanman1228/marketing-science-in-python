3.3 Quasi Experiments

Most marketing decisions don’t arrive as clean A/B tests. A new in-store display is piloted in a sample of stores before the rest of the chain. A coupon is sent to customers whose past spending crossed a threshold. An out-of-home campaign runs in one metro market while comparable markets get none.

When randomization is off the table, the real question is not which model is most sophisticated. It is what comparisons your business context actually allows: who received the treatment, when did it happen, and for what reason. The rest is about picking a quasi-experimental design that fits that structure.

Choosing the Right Method

The right method depends on how treatment was assigned and what comparisons you can actually construct. Every design comes with assumptions, so the goal is to choose the one whose assumptions you can defend in your business context. The decision tree below can help you map out the options.

Difference-in-Differences (DiD)

Here’s a concrete scenario. A retailer pilots a new in-store display for a product category in 50 stores, while 50 comparable stores keep the standard shelf layout. You measure category sales per store before and after the display rollout in both groups.

Before After Change
Pilot stores $42k $48k +$6k
Comparison stores $40k $43k +$3k
DiD estimate +$3k

The pilot stores grew by $6k, but the comparison stores also grew by $3k. That could be seasonality, traffic, or a general lift in the category. The DiD estimate is $6k minus $3k, or $3k: the incremental effect of the new display. That is the core idea behind Difference-in-Differences: compare the change over time in the treated group to the change in the untreated group, so you can strip out trends that affect everyone. The double differencing cancels out both fixed differences between groups and any shared time trends.

In code, the estimate is just those two differences:

import pandas as pd

did = pd.DataFrame({
    "group":  ["Pilot stores", "Comparison stores"],
    "before": [42, 40],   # category sales per store, $k
    "after":  [48, 43],
})
did["change"] = did["after"] - did["before"]              # +6 and +3
did_estimate = did.loc[0, "change"] - did.loc[1, "change"]
print(f"DiD estimate: ${did_estimate}k")                  # DiD estimate: $3k

The main strength of DiD is that it cancels out factors you cannot directly measure, but that only works if a key assumption holds.

The Parallel Trends Assumption

DiD relies on one critical assumption: if there had been no treatment, both groups would have followed the same trend. The focus is on trend, not starting point. The groups do not need to start at the same sales level (pilot stores can be higher than comparison stores, as in the example). What matters is that their trends were parallel before the intervention, so the control group’s change after the fact is a reasonable stand-in for what the treated group would have done without the treatment.

You can never fully verify this after the fact, since you do not observe the treated group’s counterfactual. But you can look at the pre-treatment period for a reality check.

The practical check is straightforward: plot the outcome over time for both groups and focus on the period before the intervention. If the two lines were already converging, diverging, or bending in different ways before the intervention, DiD is tough to justify. In that case, you may need to consider matching, weighting, or a different design.

If you have enough pre-treatment periods, you can back up the visual check with an event-study or a pre-trend regression. Still, this is just a credibility check, not proof of the parallel trends assumption.

Use DiD in marketing when you have store display pilots, shelf resets, regional promotion rollouts, pricing or policy changes in selected markets, app feature launches on one platform, or any situation where some units changed and others stayed the same.

Estimating DiD with a Regression

The manual calculation gives you a point estimate, but no sense of uncertainty, and it does not scale beyond two groups and two periods. In practice, you fit the equivalent regression, which gives you a standard error and works for a full panel of stores and periods. You use an indicator for the treated group, an indicator for the post period, and their interaction. The interaction term is the DiD estimate.

import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

# df: one row per store-period (store_id, period, treated, post, category_sales)
model = smf.ols("category_sales ~ treated + post + treated:post", data=df).fit(
    cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": df["store_id"]}
)
coef, se = model.params["treated:post"], model.bse["treated:post"]
print(f"DiD estimate: {coef:.2f} (SE {se:.2f})")   # DiD estimate: 3.10 (SE 0.07)

Clustering the standard error by store handles the repeated measurements for each store. You can also use this regression to check pre-trends: swap the post indicator for one indicator per period (an event-study setup) and confirm that the pre-treatment terms are close to zero. Regression Discontinuity (RDD)

RDD is useful when a business rule assigns treatment at a clear cutoff. Suppose customers with at least $500 in pre-campaign annual spend receive a coupon, while customers below $500 do not. Customers just above and just below the cutoff are likely to be similar; the main difference between them is that one group received the coupon.

In that setting, RDD compares outcomes near the cutoff. If customers who spent $502 later purchase more than those who spent $498, the jump at $500 estimates the local effect of receiving the coupon.

The key point is that RDD estimates the effect only for customers near the cutoff, not for everyone. It also depends on one condition: customers should not be able to precisely move themselves across the threshold to get the treatment.

Good marketing examples include coupons or points based on historical spend, lead-score cutoffs for sales outreach, and store-volume thresholds for trade support. Avoid cases where customers can easily move themselves across the cutoff, like a visible cart threshold.

Synthetic Control

Synthetic Control is helpful when one market, region, or store gets treated and there is no clear control group. Instead of picking a single comparison market, you build a weighted blend of untreated markets that closely matches the treated market before the intervention. That blend becomes your counterfactual.

For example, suppose a brand runs an out-of-home campaign in New York while other comparable metro markets get none. To estimate the effect on sales, you build a “synthetic New York” from a weighted mix of untreated markets that together match New York’s pre-campaign sales trend. Once the campaign starts, the gap between actual New York and its synthetic counterpart is the estimated effect.

The main credibility check is how well the synthetic version matches New York’s sales trend before the campaign. If it cannot reproduce the pre-period trend, you should not trust the post-campaign gap. The hard part is not the optimization: it is finding donor markets that were similar enough before treatment and were not indirectly affected by the campaign.

Use Synthetic Control in marketing when a single market or region gets a localized media campaign, price change, product launch, or store-format change, and you have enough history to build a credible stand-in from untreated markets.

These methods estimate the average effect of a treatment. But in practice, different customers can respond very differently. A coupon might lift purchases by 5% on average, but that average could mix customers who saw a 20% lift with others who saw no effect at all. The next chapter digs into that question.
