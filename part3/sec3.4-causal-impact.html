3.4 Causal Impact with Time Series

Every design in the previous chapter had a control group. Some stores got the display and some didn’t. One market got the campaign and the others didn’t. This chapter is for when there’s no control group at all.

A national TV campaign ran for eight weeks. Sales went up. But every market was exposed, so there’s no untreated market to compare against. What would sales have been without the campaign?

This chapter has one job: build that missing no-campaign path, and decide whether it’s credible enough to measure the campaign against. The building takes fifteen lines of code. Deciding whether to trust it is the rest of the chapter.

Building the Counterfactual

Core idea: forecast the sales you would have seen without the campaign. The effect is observed sales minus that forecast.

With no control group, the comparison has to come from time. Fit a time series model to sales before the campaign, so the campaign can’t leak into it. Project the model forward through the campaign window. That projection is the counterfactual, and the gap between it and what actually happened is the estimated effect.

The forecast can still use control series, and it’s worth being clear that these aren’t a control group. A control group is untreated units of the same kind: stores that didn’t get the display. A control series is a predictor: any series the campaign couldn’t have touched, such as a category demand index or a sibling product line with no media. It isn’t compared to the brand. It tells the forecast what the market was doing while the campaign ran.

The best-known implementation is CausalImpact, which Google released as an R package and Brodersen and colleagues described in a 2015 paper. In the causal inference literature the same design is called an interrupted time series. Python ports exist (tfp-causalimpact, and InterruptedTimeSeries in CausalPy). This chapter builds the same logic directly with statsmodels, so every assumption stays visible.

It’s the last rung of the ladder from the previous chapter, and the table shows what changed on the way up:

Three ways to build the sales you didn’t observe.
Design feature DiD Synthetic Control CausalImpact
Untreated units needed A control group A donor pool None; control series are predictors, not comparisons
The comparison comes from The control group’s change over time A weighted blend of the donor pool A forecast from the series’ own past
The assumption you can’t test Parallel trends The pre-period fit keeps holding The controls stayed untouched

The worked example is a national CPG brand: four years of weekly revenue, an eight-week TV and PR campaign, and twenty weeks of observation after it. Two control series: a subcategory demand index and a sibling product line that got no media. The data is synthetic, so the true no-campaign path is known. Before any code, the brief:

NoteAnalysis brief
  • Business question: How much incremental revenue did the campaign generate?
  • Outcome: Weekly national revenue.
  • Treatment: The eight-week TV-and-PR campaign, measured as one combined intervention.
  • Estimand: Cumulative incremental revenue over the 28 weeks after launch, including carryover and any later payback.

That last line is a decision, and it’s made now, before anyone has seen a number. It’s why the forecast below runs 28 weeks, and why the reporting-window section later in the chapter is a check rather than a choice.

Here is the code that builds the counterfactual:

import numpy as np
from statsmodels.tsa.statespace.structural import UnobservedComponents

# 1. Fit on the PRE-period only. The campaign is deliberately absent.
model = UnobservedComponents(
    np.log(y_pre),
    level="lltrend",                                   # level and slope
    freq_seasonal=[{"period": 52, "harmonics": 2}],    # annual shape
    exog=np.log(x_pre),                                # untouched controls
)
res = model.fit(disp=0)

# 2. Simulate 1,000 no-campaign paths across the 28 post-period weeks
horizon, draws = len(y_post), 1000
paths = np.exp(
    np.asarray(res.simulate(horizon, repetitions=draws, anchor="end",
                            exog=np.log(x_post))).reshape(horizon, draws)
)

# 3. Effect = observed minus counterfactual. The point estimate and the
#    interval come from the same draws, so both summarize one distribution.
counterfactual = paths.mean(axis=1)
weekly_effect = y_post - counterfactual
cumulative = weekly_effect.sum()
lo, hi = np.percentile((y_post[:, None] - paths).sum(axis=0), [2.5, 97.5])

Three choices in that code matter. The model is fit on the pre-period only, so the campaign can’t contaminate its own baseline. Sales are modelled in logs, because a campaign lift and seasonality are both multiplicative, and the simulated paths are converted back to dollars before anything is summed. And the controls enter as regressors, so the forecast knows what the market did during the campaign. Whether the model also needs a trend term, or the controls at all, isn’t a matter of taste. The next section settles it by measurement.

Validating the Counterfactual Before Measuring

This step isn’t optional. The effect you’ll report is observed minus forecast, so every dollar of forecast error becomes a dollar of fake effect. Before using the forecast to measure the campaign, you have to show it can predict a period where nothing happened. Three checks do that, and all three run on the pre-period alone.

Backtesting the forecast

Can the model predict the untreated world? Pretend the campaign started at some earlier date inside the pre-period. Fit on the data before that date, forecast the next twenty-eight weeks, and compare to what actually happened. Repeat from several dates. Twenty-eight weeks because that’s the estimand in the brief, and a model that looks fine over eight weeks can fall apart over twenty-eight.

Rolling-origin validation over nine pre-period windows where nothing happened.
Specification Forecast error Bias Interval coverage
Seasonal naive 7.96% -5.83% n/a
Level, no controls 5.54% -1.32% 99.6%
Level + controls 2.62% -0.60% 96.8%
Trend + controls 2.43% +0.48% 87.7%

The table makes the decision. Trend plus controls has the lowest error and the smallest bias, so that’s the specification we use. Two details matter along the way. The controls cut the error in half, so on this data they earn their place. And the trend term matters at this horizon: the brand grows faster than its subcategory, and a model without a slope would charge that gap to the campaign.

Placebo interventions

Does the design invent effects when nothing happened? Pick a date in the pre-period, say week 120, when there was no campaign. Pretend one started that week and run the full analysis. The right answer is zero, so whatever comes back is what this design produces from noise. That’s a placebo intervention, and the nine backtest windows above double as nine placebos. The largest placebo effect here was $636k, and one of the nine returned an interval that excluded zero. Keep both numbers. The campaign estimate has to clear them.

Checking that the controls help

Do the controls actually improve the forecast? The backtest already said yes on this data, and it’s worth seeing what that buys. In a calm world the controls narrow the interval by a factor of twelve. When a category-wide dip hits three weeks of the campaign, the estimate with controls lands within 1.3% of the truth, because the counterfactual dips with the market. Without controls the same dip is charged to the campaign, and the estimate lands 21% below the truth. That’s what a good control series does: it absorbs shocks that hit everyone, so the model doesn’t mistake them for the campaign.

It only works if the campaign didn’t touch the control. That’s the assumption that can’t be tested from the data, and it gets its own section, When Not to Trust the Estimate.

Estimating the Campaign Effect

Only now does the campaign window get used. The chosen specification is fit on the full pre-period and projected forward. The result is the three-panel chart that has become the standard way to present a one-shot campaign: observed against counterfactual, the weekly gap, and the running total.

Choosing the reporting window

The brief fixed the estimand at 28 weeks. Here’s why that mattered. Look at the cumulative panel: the curve climbs during the eight-week flight, keeps climbing for a few weeks after it as the carryover runs, then turns down as demand that was pulled forward gets paid back. Three windows, each of which sounds reasonable, give three different numbers.

The same model and the same campaign, read over three windows.
Reporting window Estimate 95% interval Truth Error
Flight only, 8 weeks $1,157k $844k to $1,446k $1,291k -10.4%
Flight and carryover, 12 weeks $1,603k $1,174k to $1,996k $1,574k +1.8%
Full post window, 28 weeks $1,164k $411k to $1,890k $1,124k +3.6%

All three estimates are accurate for the window they cover. Only the last one answers the brief, because only it counts the payback. Stopping at week 12, the peak of the curve, overstates the full answer by 38%. The model wasn’t wrong. The window was. That’s why the window goes in the brief, before anyone looks at the curve.

How much to trust the interval

The band on the chart holds the fitted model fixed and propagates its noise forward. That makes it a floor on the real uncertainty, not the whole of it. Two things help you read it. First, the placebo results: the $1,164k estimate is larger than every placebo effect this design produced, and the biggest of those was $636k. That’s a more useful statement than a p-value, because it’s in the same units as the decision. Second, the audit below.

NoteDeep dive: how good is this design, really?

The notebook re-runs the chosen specification on two hundred fresh synthetic datasets and grades it against the truth each time. The nominal 95% interval covers the truth 90% of the time. A genuine effect is flagged as significant 60% of the time. When the true effect is zero, it’s called significant 9% of the time. That isn’t a weak model. It’s a weak measurement situation: one treated series, no randomization, an effect that’s small next to the noise. If the decision needs more than that, run a geo experiment next time (Chapter 6.3).

When Not to Trust the Estimate

Three things break this analysis. Two of them leave no trace in the data.

The campaign touched a control

The more predictive a control is, the more likely the campaign reached it. The subcategory index carries most of the model’s explanatory power, which is exactly why a national campaign for a large brand in that subcategory is a threat to it. Leak 35% of the brand’s lift into the index and the estimate keeps only 65% of the true effect, with an interval that no longer excludes zero. A campaign that worked now reads as inconclusive. And every diagnostic passes: the pre-period fit is clean, the interval is tight, the backtest looks the same. The violated assumption lives outside the data. The only way to check it is to ask the people who ran the campaign what it could have touched.

The pre-period relationship broke

A relationship that held for four years can still stop. Suppose a competitor enters and grows the subcategory by 5% in the post-period, and this brand doesn’t share in it. The controls now say the market got better, the counterfactual rises, and the estimate comes back at negative $460k against a true effect of positive $1,124k. It’s the only scenario in the chapter where the truth falls outside the interval, and it isn’t testable from pre-period data by construction. This is the same discipline DiD demands: the output is only as good as the claim that the controls kept behaving the way they did before.

The forecast was never good enough

The third failure does leave a trace, if you look. Run the same machinery on real Dunnhumby store data: take one store’s weekly sales, multiply the final twelve weeks by 1.15, and try to recover the planted lift with four other stores as controls.

The lift comes back. The planted 1,775 units are recovered as 1,833, with an interval that excludes zero. It would be easy to write that up as a successful controlled analysis.

It isn’t one. The cross-store correlation that justified the controls was 0.45, but the Dunnhumby panel starts small and grows, so every store rises together for reasons that have nothing to do with demand. Trim that onboarding ramp and the correlation falls to 0.01. The backtest settles it: adding the four control stores moves forecast error from 13.7% to 13.8%, which is to say not at all. The narrower interval is the trap. Extra regressors soak up in-sample variation and tighten the band even when the forecast didn’t improve. A narrower interval isn’t evidence that the controls helped. A better backtest is. What produced the number was a projection of one store’s own recent level, and a 13.7% forecast error is too weak to carry a causal claim. Saying so is the finding.

A Practical Workflow

The chapter compresses to eight steps. Run them in order.

  1. Write the brief. Business question, outcome, treatment, and the estimand with its reporting window. Fix it before you see a number.
  2. Choose the control series. They have to be series the campaign couldn’t touch. Ask the people who ran it.
  3. Fit on the pre-period only.
  4. Backtest the counterfactual at the horizon you’ll report. Pick the specification from the backtest, not from taste.
  5. Run placebo interventions. Know what the design returns from noise before you read the real number.
  6. Estimate the post-period effect over the window you fixed in step 1.
  7. Stress-test the controls. Could the campaign have reached them? Could the pre-period relationship have broken?
  8. If the counterfactual is weak, don’t report a causal number. A 13.7% backtest error is a forecast, not a measurement.

CausalImpact doesn’t turn observational data into an experiment. It gives you a structured way to ask whether a time series can support a credible counterfactual. When it can’t, the answer isn’t a better model. It’s a better experiment next time (Chapter 6.3).

Key Takeaways

  1. When there’s no control group, forecast the no-campaign path from the series’ own past and from controls the campaign couldn’t touch. The effect is observed minus forecast.
  2. Validate the forecast before you measure anything. Backtests and placebo dates inside the pre-period tell you whether the model can predict a world where nothing happened, and what it returns from noise alone.
  3. The reporting window is a bigger lever than the model. Stopping at the peak of the cumulative curve overstated the full-window answer by 38%. Fix the window before you see a number.
  4. Two failures leave no trace in the data: a control the campaign touched, and a pre-period relationship that broke. The third, a forecast that was never good enough, shows up in the backtest. A narrower interval doesn’t prove the controls helped. A better backtest does.
