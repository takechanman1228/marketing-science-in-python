5.2 Product Assortment Optimization

The category review is on the calendar: the planogram team wants shelf space back, and the category manager has to name the SKUs to give up. An underperforming product does more than take up room: it ties up inventory, complicates the supply chain, and makes the shelf harder to shop. The decision is which products to cut, which to keep, and how to validate those calls before scaling them across the chain.

This chapter runs that decision on two years of grocery transactions: screen the long tail by revenue, check whether the demand would stay in the store, and hand back a shortlist ranked by how safe each SKU is to test.

Companion notebook: sec5.2_assortment_optimization.ipynb reproduces the revenue ABC screen, substitution check, and action assignment on the Dunnhumby Complete Journey grocery dataset.
View on GitHub

NoteWhy revenue, not margin?

This chapter analyzes revenue because the public Dunnhumby Complete Journey dataset does not include product cost or COGS. The same workflow runs on gross margin or contribution margin when production teams have cost data; only the business interpretation changes, from revenue-risk management to margin optimization.

Why Cutting Is Hard

Assortments grow for good reasons: buyers want broad coverage and brands keep launching variants. A leaner shelf can also be easier to shop, though the choice-overload literature is mixed on how large that effect is (see Further Reading). The asymmetry is organizational: the cost of one more SKU is diffuse and easy to overlook, while removing a product is a visible and sometimes unpopular move. Assortment optimization is ultimately a decision process. The role of the data is to give the team a defensible reason to act.

A Workflow for Assortment Optimization

The workflow answers two questions in sequence:

  1. Screening → candidate pool. Which products contribute little revenue and sit in the long tail?
  2. Substitution check → safety assessment. For each candidate, if we remove it, does the demand stay in our store?

A candidate with plausible substitutes among higher-performing products is a lower-risk test candidate. Without them, test before acting. Nothing is confirmed safe until a matched-store test validates it.

Step 1: Screening SKUs by Revenue

Before deciding what to remove, we need to narrow the SKU universe down. A grocery store may carry thousands of products, but a small fraction drives most of the revenue, while the long-tail items each bring in very little. That long tail is where removal candidates come from.

Retail teams often use a Pareto-style ranking called ABC analysis. Products are sorted by their contribution, usually sales value or margin, though sometimes by units, and then split into three tiers.

  • A items (top ~20%): the vital few. They drive roughly 80% of the chosen metric. Protect, monitor, and optimize these.
  • B items (next ~30%): the important middle. Steady contributors that need less attention.
  • C items (bottom ~50%): the long tail. Individually small, collectively noisy. Candidates for rationalization.

C-rank products are where you start building your candidate pool. Not every C-rank item should be removed, but any product you consider cutting should come from this group.

In code, rank SKUs by cumulative revenue share, cut into A/B/C tiers, and take the C-rank long tail as the candidate pool:

ranked = sku_summary.sort_values("revenue", ascending=False)["revenue"]
# Classify on the cumulative share *before* each SKU, so the SKU that crosses
# the 80% line still counts toward A (and a single dominant SKU stays A).
cum_before = (ranked.cumsum() / ranked.sum()).shift(fill_value=0)
sku_summary["abc_revenue"] = pd.cut(cum_before, bins=[0, 0.80, 0.95, 1.0],
                                    labels=["A", "B", "C"], right=False)

# Candidate pool = C-rank by revenue (the long tail)
candidates = sku_summary[sku_summary["abc_revenue"] == "C"]

The full function, covering zero/negative-sales filtering and NaN-safe binning, is in the companion notebook.

Figure 1 shows the revenue Pareto curve for about 39,000 Dunnhumby grocery SKUs (after dropping 38 with no sales). Revenue is highly concentrated: fewer than 20% of SKUs generate 80% of sales, while the C-rank pool (abc_revenue == "C") contains 63% of SKUs but only 5% of revenue. This long tail forms the candidate pool. It is worth examining, not defending, based on volume alone.

C-rank revenue gets a product into the candidate pool, but not all the way to removal. Step 2 determines which candidates are safe to test and which require more caution.

Step 2: Substitution check, “Will the demand stay?”

For each C-rank candidate, the question is: if we remove it, will customers buy something else from us, or leave the store?

Within the same sub-category, products that rarely appear in the same basket may be substitutes. Shoppers usually buy one brand of yogurt, not three. We use low basket co-occurrence, measured by Lift, as a proxy for substitution:

\[\text{Lift}(A, B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A) \times P(B)}\]

Lift > 1 means two products tend to be bought together (complements); Lift < 1 means they rarely are, which within a sub-category suggests they substitute for each other, and Lift = 0 (never in the same basket) is the strongest signal. The same-subcategory constraint matters: across categories, low co-occurrence just means the products serve different needs (cereal vs. pasta), not that they substitute.

This is a proxy, not proof. Low co-occurrence can also reflect different shopping occasions or customer segments.

For each C-rank candidate, the check scans its same-subcategory peers (restricted to A/B-revenue SKUs that are large enough to absorb redirected demand) and flags the ones with low Lift:

co_occur = len(cand_baskets & peer_baskets)
if co_occur == 0:                       # never bought together → strongest signal
    subs.append({"substitute": peer_id, "lift": 0.0})
else:
    lift = (co_occur / total_baskets) / (p_cand * p_peer)
    if lift < lift_threshold:           # low Lift within a sub-category → likely substitute
        subs.append({"substitute": peer_id, "lift": round(lift, 3)})

The full function, covering sub-category grouping, the A/B-revenue substitute pool, and minimum-basket guards, is in the companion notebook.

There are two optional reinforcements. Positive cross-price elasticity, when available, supports substitution. When basket data is sparse, such as for new products or small stores, attribute similarity, like brand, price tier, or use case, can be a practical fallback.

The output: a scored candidate list

The long tail is too big to score whole, so the notebook scores 150 C-rank SKUs: the 100 bought most often plus the C-rank SKUs in the same sub-categories, all with at least 30 baskets over the two years. A SKU bought a dozen times cannot carry a substitution signal either way. Steps 1 and 2 then assign one of three actions. Table 1 shows one real candidate per action (sub-category labels cleaned up for reading):

Across the 150, the actions split 105 / 28 / 17. Most of the examined tail has an in-category substitute and no special basket role, and for these zero co-occurrence (a Lift of 0.0, the strongest substitution signal in transaction data) is the common case. This is not a remove list. It is a shortlist, sorted by how safe each candidate is to test first.

A candidate with a clear in-category substitute is the safest to test for removal. The 17 with no clear substitute are the opposite case: fewer than five A/B-revenue peers could absorb the demand, so it may leave the store. Keep the product for now and look for ways to lower its cost, such as smaller packs or better supplier terms, or test carefully before removing.

The 28 in the middle are a guardrail for halo effects. A low-priced SKU that often appears with higher-value items may help attract those baskets, so removing it could reduce sales elsewhere. Investigate these cases before cutting, rather than relying on revenue alone. In dense grocery data, almost every SKU co-occurs with something, so use this flag only for the clearest cases. Also, not every low-revenue SKU should be in the candidate pool. Some products earn their shelf space by driving store trips, anchoring price image, or signaling category credibility, roles that revenue and Lift do not capture.

Validate before scaling

The scored list is a set of hypotheses, not a removal list. Before rolling out a deletion, run a small matched-store test: remove the SKU in treatment stores, keep comparable control stores unchanged, and monitor sub-category revenue, revenue per basket, and basket count.

Do not evaluate the removed SKU’s own sales; they will be zero by construction. The question is whether demand stays in the category and in the basket. If the result is inconclusive, do not scale the removal.

Key Takeaways

  • Revenue ABC produces candidates, not decisions. Low same-subcategory co-occurrence is a proxy for substitution, not proof, and a cheap SKU that anchors valuable baskets can be worth keeping.
  • Never roll out without a matched-store test. Measure sub-category revenue, revenue per basket, and basket count, not the removed SKU’s own sales. An inconclusive result is not a green light.
