5.2 Product Assortment Optimization

Shelf space in physical retail is always at a premium. An underperforming product does more than just take up room: it ties up inventory, complicates the supply chain, and makes the shelf harder for customers to shop. The real challenge is figuring out which products to keep, which to cut, and how to validate those decisions before scaling them across the business. Here, I’ll walk through a practical workflow for making those calls and testing them in a way that reduces risk.

NoteWhy revenue, not margin?

This chapter analyses revenue because the public Dunnhumby Complete Journey dataset does not include product cost or COGS. The same workflow runs on gross margin or contribution margin when production teams have cost data; only the business interpretation changes, from revenue-risk management to margin optimisation.

The “More SKUs, More Revenue” Fallacy

A typical grocery store stocks around 50,000 SKUs. Trader Joe’s, by contrast, carries only about 4,000, roughly one-tenth as many, but generates nearly twice the revenue per square foot as Whole Foods. While Trader Joe’s also relies on private label and smaller store formats, a leaner assortment is a key factor. Fewer products allow for tighter inventory, simpler supply chains, and less decision fatigue for customers. Psychologist Barry Schwartz calls this the paradox of choice: past a certain point, more options don’t help customers; they overwhelm them, delay purchases, and reduce satisfaction.

Most retailers face the opposite challenge. There are good reasons to add SKUs: buyers want broad coverage and brands keep launching new variants. The problem is that the cost of adding another SKU is diffuse and often overlooked, while removing a product is a visible and sometimes unpopular move. Assortment optimization is ultimately a decision process. The main role of data here is to help teams move past organizational inertia and make more informed choices.

A Workflow for Assortment Optimization

The workflow answers two questions in sequence:

  1. Screening → candidate pool. Which products contribute little revenue and sit in the long tail?
  2. Substitution check → safety assessment. For each candidate, if we remove it, does the demand stay in our store?

A candidate with plausible substitutes among higher-performing products is a lower-risk test candidate. Without them, test before acting. Nothing is confirmed safe until a matched-store test validates it.

Step 1: Screening SKUs by Revenue

Before deciding what to remove, we need to narrow the SKU universe down. A grocery store may carry thousands of products, but a small fraction drives most of the revenue, while a long tail each brings in very little. That long tail is where removal candidates come from.

Retail teams often use a Pareto-style ranking called ABC analysis. Products are sorted by their contribution, usually sales value or margin, though sometimes by units, and then split into three tiers.

  • A items (top ~20%): the vital few. They drive roughly 80% of the chosen metric. Protect, monitor, and optimise these.
  • B items (next ~30%): the important middle. Steady contributors that need less attention.
  • C items (bottom ~50%): the long tail. Individually small, collectively noisy. Candidates for rationalisation.

C-rank products are where you start building your candidate pool. Not every C-rank item should be removed, but any product you consider cutting should come from this group.

NoteImplementation sketch: revenue_abc

Rank SKUs by cumulative revenue share, cut into A/B/C tiers, and take the C-rank long tail as the candidate pool.

cumulative = df.sort_values("revenue", ascending=False)["revenue"].cumsum() / df["revenue"].sum()
df["abc_revenue"] = pd.cut(cumulative, bins=[0, 0.80, 0.95, 1.0], labels=["A", "B", "C"])

# Candidate pool = C-rank by revenue (the long tail)
candidates = sku_summary[sku_summary["abc_revenue"] == "C"]

Figure 1 shows the revenue Pareto curve for about 39,000 Dunnhumby grocery SKUs (after dropping 38 with no sales). Revenue is highly concentrated: fewer than 20% of SKUs generate 80% of sales, while the C-rank pool (abc_revenue == "C") contains 63% of SKUs but only 5% of revenue. This long tail forms the candidate pool. It is worth examining, not defending, based on volume alone.

C-rank revenue gets a product into the candidate pool, but not all the way to removal. Step 2 determines which candidates are safe to test and which require more caution.

Step 2: Substitution check, “Will the demand stay?”

For each C-rank candidate, the question is: if we remove it, will customers buy something else from us, or leave the store?

Within the same sub-category, products that rarely appear in the same basket may be substitutes. Shoppers usually buy one brand of yogurt, not three. We use low basket co-occurrence, measured by Lift, as a proxy for substitution:

\[\text{Lift}(A, B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A) \times P(B)}\]

Lift > 1 means two products tend to be bought together (complements); Lift < 1 means they rarely are, which within a sub-category suggests they substitute for each other, and Lift = 0 (never in the same basket) is the strongest signal. The same-subcategory constraint matters: across categories, low co-occurrence just means the products serve different needs (cereal vs. pasta), not that they substitute.

This is a proxy, not proof. Low co-occurrence can also reflect different shopping occasions or customer segments.

NoteImplementation sketch: build_substitution_map

For each C-rank candidate, scan its same-subcategory peers (restricted to A/B-revenue SKUs that are large enough to absorb redirected demand) and flag the ones with low Lift. Zero co-occurrence is the strongest substitution signal.

co_occur = len(cand_baskets & peer_baskets)
if co_occur == 0:                       # never bought together → strongest signal
    subs.append({"substitute": peer_id, "lift": 0.0})
else:
    lift = (co_occur / total_baskets) / (p_cand * p_peer)
    if lift < lift_threshold:           # low Lift within a sub-category → likely substitute
        subs.append({"substitute": peer_id, "lift": round(lift, 3)})

The full function, covering sub-category grouping, the A/B-revenue substitute pool (high_rank_skus), and minimum-basket guards, is in the companion script.

There are two optional reinforcements. Positive cross-price elasticity, when available, supports substitution. When basket data is sparse, such as for new products or small stores, attribute similarity, like brand, price tier, or use case, can be a practical fallback.

The output: a scored candidate list

Steps 1 and 2 produce a per-candidate table like this:

SKU ABC (Rev) Substitute? Action
Budget cereal C → Store brand cereal (Lift 0.6) Test for removal
500g yogurt C → 1kg yogurt (Lift 0.0) Test for removal
Niche imported cheese C (none) Keep or test carefully
Loss-leader snack C → Store-brand snack (Lift 0.5) Investigate before cutting

A Lift of 0.0 means the two products never appeared together in a basket. This is the strongest substitution signal available in transaction data, though it is not definitive by itself. Values between 0 and the threshold (default 0.8) are weaker but still suggest substitution.

This does not produce a remove list. It produces a shortlist, sorted by how safe each candidate is to test first.

Candidate type Action
C-revenue SKU with clear substitute Test for removal
C-revenue SKU with no substitute Keep or test carefully
C-revenue SKU tied to valuable baskets Investigate before cutting

The first two rows come directly from the substitution check. A candidate with a clear in-category substitute is the safest to test for removal. If there is no substitute, demand may leave the store. In that case, keep the product for now and look for ways to lower its cost, such as smaller packs or better supplier terms, or test carefully before removing.

The third row is a guardrail for halo effects. A low-priced SKU that often appears with higher-value items may help attract those baskets, so removing it could reduce sales elsewhere. Investigate these cases before cutting, rather than relying on revenue alone. In dense grocery data, almost every SKU co-occurs with something, so use this flag only for the clearest cases. Also, not every low-revenue SKU should be in the candidate pool. Some products earn their shelf space by driving store trips, anchoring price image, or signaling category credibility, roles that revenue and Lift do not capture.

Validate before scaling

The scored list is a set of hypotheses, not a removal list. Before rolling out a deletion, run a small matched-store test: remove the SKU in treatment stores, keep comparable control stores unchanged, and monitor sub-category revenue, revenue per basket, and basket count.

Do not evaluate the removed SKU’s own sales; they will be zero by construction. The question is whether demand stays in the category and in the basket. If the result is inconclusive, do not scale the removal.

Key Takeaways

  • This is revenue-risk management, not profit optimization. Without product cost in the dataset, revenue stands in as the candidate signal. Teams with COGS should swap in gross margin or contribution margin; the workflow is unchanged.

  • Revenue ABC finds candidates, it doesn’t make decisions. The C-rank long tail is where you look, but a low-revenue SKU only becomes a removal candidate after the substitution check.

  • Low same-subcategory co-occurrence (Lift) is a proxy for substitution, not proof. It says customers rarely buy both in one trip, consistent with substitution, but also with different occasions or segments. Treat it as a hypothesis to test.

  • Watch for halo effects, but don’t over-apply them. A cheap SKU tied to valuable baskets can be worth keeping. In dense basket data weak co-occurrence is everywhere, so flag only the clear cases.

  • Never roll out without a matched-store test. Measure sub-category revenue, revenue per basket, and basket count, not the removed product’s own sales. An inconclusive result is not a green light.
