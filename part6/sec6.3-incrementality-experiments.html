6.3 Incrementality Experiments

The MMM in Section 6.2 recommended cutting Google Search by 30%. Should we just do it? Not yet. Search spend barely changed in three years, so the model never saw what happens when it moves. Its estimate of what the next Search dollar earns runs from 0.4 to 9.5.

What we need to know is Search’s incrementality: the revenue that would not have happened without the ads. Someone who searches for the store by name and clicks the ad might have clicked the ordinary result just below it and bought anyway. The purchase record cannot tell the two cases apart.

The way to find out is an experiment. We cut Search by 30% in some markets, leave it unchanged in similar markets, and compare revenue. The unchanged markets show what would have happened without the cut, which is called the counterfactual. This design is a geo experiment.

The result is reported as incremental ROAS (iROAS): the change in revenue divided by the change in spend. For a cut, it is the revenue lost per dollar saved.

Companion notebook: sec6.3_geo_experiment.ipynb runs the experiment on a synthetic 40-market panel. The true effect is planted in the data, so every estimate below can be checked against it.

Designing the Test

Three things are decided before the test starts: which markets to compare, what result would justify the cut, and how long to run.

Which markets. The test compares pairs of similar markets. Each market is paired with one of similar size and sales history, for example two states with similar sales, and a coin flip decides which of the two gets the cut. Pairing similar markets reduces noise. The coin flip is randomization: because we don’t choose which market gets the cut, a difference between the two can be attributed to the cut.

The panel has 40 markets with 96 weeks of history. The notebook keeps the 16 whose revenue tracks the rest of the business most closely, sorts them by size, and forms eight matched pairs (Figure 1). In each pair, one market cuts Search by 30% and the other keeps spending as usual.

The paired markets should not share shoppers or media. If people who see the ad in one market shop in the control market next door, part of the effect leaks into the control group and the estimate shrinks toward zero. This is called spillover, and it cannot be corrected in the analysis.

What result justifies the cut. The cut is worth making if the revenue it loses is worth less than the money it saves. With a contribution margin of \(m\), a dollar of spend breaks even when it returns \(1/m\) of revenue. At a 40% margin, a Search dollar has to bring back $2.50. This is the break-even iROAS.

We write the decision rule before the test, using the 95% confidence interval for iROAS:

  • Entirely below 2.50: cut.
  • Entirely above 2.50: keep the budget.
  • Straddling 2.50: hold the national plan and run a follow-up test.

How long to run. The required length depends on the size of the cut and on how noisy the markets are. A large cut is visible within a few weeks. A small cut takes longer to separate from ordinary week-to-week movement. A 20-50% spend change over four to eight weeks is a common starting point.

The notebook checks our design by simulation. It pretends the test launched on past dates, plants a known iROAS in the history, and counts how often the analysis finds it. The share of runs that find the effect is the test’s power. The smallest effect found 80% of the time is the minimum detectable effect (MDE). A 20% cut over four weeks has an MDE of 0.70. A 30% cut over eight weeks has an MDE of 0.40, so we use that design.

MDE measures whether the test can tell an effect from zero. Our decision depends on telling it from 2.50, so the notebook also simulates the decision rule. When the true iROAS is 1.5, the rule returns “cut” 99% of the time. When it is 2.0, it returns “cut” only 21% of the time and “hold” otherwise. If the true value is close to break-even, the test would need to be longer or larger.

It helps to keep the two thresholds apart. An iROAS too small to tell from zero would still give an interval well below 2.50, and that would be enough to justify the cut.

All of these choices are fixed before launch: the market list, the dates, the spend plan, the estimator, the break-even, and the random seed. If the design can change after the results are in, it can be adjusted toward the answer someone prefers.

Measuring the Effect with Difference-in-Differences

The test starts on February 5, 2024. Search spend drops 30% in the eight test markets for eight weeks, and nothing else changes in those markets. During the test we record the spend actually delivered, because iROAS is calculated from the spend that was really removed. We also wait until the eight weeks are over before reading the result, because an early look is noisier and produces more false positives.

Why a before-and-after comparison fails. The simplest analysis compares revenue in the test markets during the test with the eight weeks before it. That gives an iROAS of 0.44, which would mean the cut costs only 44 cents of revenue per dollar saved. But revenue was rising in every market during those weeks because of the season, including markets where nothing changed. The seasonal rise offset most of the loss from the cut, so the before-and-after number is far too small.

Difference-in-Differences. The control markets went through the same season without the cut. Difference-in-Differences (DiD), introduced in Section 3.3, uses them to remove the seasonal movement. It takes the revenue change in each test market and subtracts the change in its paired control:

\[ \text{effect} = (\text{test}_{\text{after}} - \text{test}_{\text{before}}) - (\text{control}_{\text{after}} - \text{control}_{\text{before}}) \]

In a regression, this effect is the coefficient on the interaction of treat and post:

import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

model = smf.ols("revenue ~ treat * post", data=analysis).fit(
    cov_type="cluster",
    cov_kwds={"groups": analysis["pair"]},   # one coin flip per pair
)

The coefficient shows that each test market lost about $9,900 of revenue per week relative to its control. Over eight markets and eight weeks, the cut lost $633,069 of revenue and saved $421,906 of Search spend. Dividing the two gives an iROAS of 1.50, with a standard error of 0.257 and a 95% confidence interval of [0.892, 2.109].

Because the data are synthetic, we can compare this with the truth: the iROAS planted in the panel is 1.500. Two further checks in the notebook give the same answer. A synthetic control, which builds a weighted blend of untreated markets to match the test markets before launch, gives 1.486 (Figure 2). A placebo test, which swaps test and control within each pair, finds an estimate as large as ours in only 2 of the 256 possible assignments (p = 0.008).

Comparing with break-even. Each dollar cut from Search lost about $1.50 of revenue, and break-even is $2.50. The entire confidence interval is below 2.50, so by the rule written before the test, the cut is justified. In dollar terms, saving $100 of Search spend lost about $150 of revenue, which carried $60 of contribution at a 40% margin. The business is $40 better off.

The effect is statistically significant (p = 0.008), so Search ads do generate revenue. At the current spend level they generate less than they cost, and the decision depends on that comparison.

The experiment covered a 30% cut over eight weeks in eight markets, which hold 19.9% of national Search spend. It tells us that the last 30% of Search spend does not pay for itself. It does not tell us where the saved budget should go, because no other channel was changed. Section 6.4 takes this result back to the MMM to answer that.

Key Takeaways

  • Incrementality is the revenue that would not have happened without the ads. A geo experiment measures it by changing spend in some markets and using similar markets as the counterfactual.
  • Decide the markets, the decision rule, and the test length before launch, and check by simulation that the test can tell the iROAS from break-even, not only from zero.
  • Judge the result against break-even iROAS, not against zero. Search’s ads clearly generated revenue, but at 1.50 they returned less than they cost, so the cut is justified.
