6.4 Calibrating MMM with Experiments

Section 6.3 measured what the model in Section 6.2 could only estimate. A 30% cut to Google Search in eight test markets cost 0.200 of revenue per dollar removed, with a 95% interval of [0.040, 0.361]. The model had put Search’s ROAS at 3.53, with a 90% credible interval from 0.39 to 10.29. Read side by side, that looks like the model was off by a factor of eighteen. Was it?

No, and there are two reasons. The first is estimand. The experiment measured the return on the last Search dollars, the ones a 30% cut removes; the model’s headline is the return on all of them, averaged from zero. Ask the model the experiment’s own question, what a 30% cut over eight weeks costs, and it answers 2.54 with a 90% credible interval of [0.28, 7.21]. That closes the gap from eighteenfold to thirteen, and the two intervals touch: the experiment’s reaches up to 0.361, the model’s down to 0.28. It does not close it.

The second reason is that the model’s Search number was never really evidence. Search spend barely moved in three years of history, so the likelihood had little to say, and the posterior is mostly the prior with a seasonal tilt: a prior wearing a posterior’s clothes. On the model’s own numbers Search cleared break-even, 2.83 marginal against a hurdle of 2.50, and was still the weakest of the five channels, which is why the optimizer cut it. The experiment says it clears neither bar. The model’s prediction was recorded before the test so that the test could grade it. The grade is that the model was too uncertain to act on and, at the edge of its own interval, wrong. The experiment is the first design-based causal evidence about Search, and its uncertainty is small relative to the decision it has to support.

Because the outcome in this example is revenue, Meridian’s ROI is numerically a revenue ROAS; the prose says ROAS and the code keeps Meridian’s names. Calibration is how that evidence enters the model: translate the experiment into a prior, refit, and see what changed. The point is not to make the model agree with the experiment. It’s to find out whether better causal evidence changes the decision, and if it doesn’t, why not.

Why Calibrate?

An MMM sees the whole picture: every channel, the full history, the shape of each response curve. But it sees it observationally. When channels flight together, or media rises with promotions and seasonal demand, the likelihood can’t tell which one moved sales, and the posterior for a channel’s ROAS stays wide even when the model predicts revenue well. That is what happened to Search in Section 6.2: a good fit, and an interval too wide to act on.

An experiment sees one thing, at one operating point, for one window, but it sees it causally. It’s an anchor, not a replacement. Put the anchor in the model and the model’s other strengths, the other channels, the curve, the history, do the rest. That is why Section 6.3’s evidence package carried the estimand and the uncertainty, and not just the number.

Why You Cannot Paste 0.20 into the Model

Meridian’s roi_m parameter is average ROAS: a channel’s total incremental revenue divided by its total spend. The experiment measured marginal ROAS: the slope of the response curve at the current spend level. Section 6.2 showed why the two differ on a saturating curve; Figure 2 puts them on one picture. Early dollars are more productive than later ones, so average ROAS exceeds marginal ROAS wherever the curve has begun to bend.

So what happens if you set an average-ROAS prior at 0.20 anyway? The notebook does it. The posterior average ROAS obediently moves to 0.21. But the model still has a curve, and a curve whose average is 0.20 has a marginal below that. The calibrated model’s marginal ROAS for Search comes out at 0.17, against the 0.200 the experiment measured and the 0.213 planted in the data. The optimizer works from marginal ROAS, so the error lands exactly where the decision is made.

Here the miss is 14%, small enough to look harmless. That is a property of this channel, not of the mistake. The size of the error is the gap between a channel’s average and marginal return, and Search’s curve is gentle: its true marginal is 0.61 of its true average. Meta’s ratio is 0.75, TV/CTV’s is 0.43, TikTok’s is 0.27. Make the same substitution on TikTok and the model would be wrong by a factor of nearly four. The lesson is not that the mistake is small. It is that its size is set by the channel you happen to make it on.

Warning

An experiment’s estimand has to match the parameter it calibrates. The Section 6.3 test measured marginal ROAS. Meridian has a parameterization for exactly that.

Turning the Experiment into a Prior

Meridian can place the prior on marginal ROI instead of average ROI. Its definition is local: the return on a one percent increase in spend along the observed spend path. The pull-back measured a wider contrast, the block between 70% and 100% of spend, from the other side of the same operating point. That is much closer to marginal ROI than to average ROI, but it isn’t the same number, so the experiment anchors the mROI prior rather than observing it exactly. In this data the two sit within a rounding error for Search, 0.213 against 0.200, because Search’s curve is gentle and its carryover short; for TV/CTV they would not, 1.80 against 0.83.

Three adjustments come from Google’s own documentation, and one is ours. Duration is the only one that moves the center: a short window misses carryover, so the point estimate is divided by the share of the channel’s adstock weights the window captured. Search’s carryover is short, a one-week half-life, so an eight-week window captures 99.6% of it and the center barely moves; the same window on a six-week half-life channel like TV/CTV would capture 78%, and the correction would be real.

The rest widen rather than shift. Spend scale compares the experiment’s average daily spend rate with the channel’s. The eight test markets ran at about a fifth of the national rate, and the penalty grows as that fraction falls, so it dominates everything else here. Recency discounts old evidence on a 52-week half-life; this test ran six weeks after the modeled period ended, so it is not stale and the penalty is zero. A year-old result would carry a materially wider prior. The fourth adjustment is ours, not Google’s: the experiment averaged the curve across a 30% block while the prior describes the slope at today’s spend. The model has an opinion about that gap, but the opinion comes from the fit the experiment exists to correct, so it widens the prior rather than moving it. The notebook computes each term and prints it. Together they leave the center at 0.201 and take the standard error from 0.075 to 0.161. Almost all of that comes from the spend-scale term: the experiment’s own precision was fine, and what widened the prior is that eight markets are a fifth of the channel.

Note

Google’s Meridian documentation describes a CalibrationBuilder that applies spend, recency, and duration adjustments and builds ROI priors from lift experiments whose counterfactual is zero spend. We don’t use it mechanically here: this experiment has a non-zero counterfactual and estimates a 30% local contrast, which is the estimand mismatch its own documentation warns about. Instead the notebook makes the translation assumptions explicit and tests whether the decision is stable to them. The notebook pins Meridian 1.6.2.

In this parameterization every channel’s prior is stated the same way, so the other four channels keep a weak marginal-ROI prior: LogNormal(-0.49, 0.90). That is Section 6.2’s weak ROAS prior restated on the marginal scale, since posterior marginal return runs about half of posterior average return on this panel. Resist the urge to make it flatter. On a saturating curve a diffuse prior on marginal ROI leaves average ROI unbounded above, and the uncalibrated channels come back with credible intervals wide enough to be useless.

import numpy as np
import tensorflow as tf
import tensorflow_probability as tfp
from meridian import constants
from meridian.model import prior_distribution, spec

channels = list(data.media_channel.values)
target = channels.index("Google Search")

# Weak marginal-ROI prior for every channel, then the experiment on Search.
mroi_mu = np.full(len(channels), 0.2 + np.log(0.5))
mroi_sigma = np.full(len(channels), 0.9)
mroi_mu[target], mroi_sigma[target] = -1.8542, 0.7050   # centre 0.201, SE 0.161

prior = prior_distribution.PriorDistribution(
    mroi_m=tfp.distributions.LogNormal(
        loc=tf.constant(mroi_mu, dtype=tf.float32),
        scale=tf.constant(mroi_sigma, dtype=tf.float32),
        name=constants.MROI_M,
    )
)

model_spec = spec.ModelSpec(prior=prior, media_prior_type="mroi", max_lag=12)

Refit rather than editing posterior summaries after the fact. The calibrated channel should move toward the evidence and become better identified, but it needn’t land exactly on 0.200. The likelihood, the curve, and the prior all contribute. Tightening one channel also redistributes contribution among correlated channels, so look at every channel and the total prediction, not only Search.

Tip

PyMC-Marketing’s add_lift_test_measurements takes the other route: it adds the lift observation as a likelihood term tied to the response curve, with the spend change stated explicitly. For a heavy-up or pull-back test that is a direct fit. Choose by estimand alignment and model structure, not by package.

Negative and near-zero lift are valid outcomes. A LogNormal prior can’t express evidence centered at or below zero. Use a family with the right support rather than clipping the estimate to a small positive number.

What Changed?

The model now carries the experiment. Three fits make the comparison honest: the Section 6.2 model as a reference, a control fit that switches to marginal-ROI priors without the experiment, and the calibrated fit. Only the last one has seen the geo test, so the difference between the last two is the evidence and nothing else.

Start with the one line where the model and the experiment measure the same thing: what a 30% Search cut over eight weeks costs.

Google Search 6.2 reference Before (control) After (calibrated)
Predicted cost of the tested cut 2.54 [0.28, 7.21] 1.57 [0.15, 5.52] 0.20 [0.05, 0.49]
Average ROAS 3.53 [0.39, 10.29] 2.55 [0.21, 9.25] 0.27 [0.06, 0.71]
Marginal ROAS 2.83 [0.31, 8.28] 1.76 [0.17, 6.36] 0.22 [0.05, 0.54]
Relative half-width 1.41 1.76 1.13
Guardrail HOLD HOLD HOLD
Optimizer move −30.0% −30.0% −30.0%

The experiment measured 0.200. The planted truth is 0.213 marginal, 0.35 average, 0.200 for the tested cut.

The point estimate lands where it should. Search’s marginal return moves from 1.76 to 0.22, within a hundredth of the planted truth, and the model’s prediction for the tested cut goes from 1.57 to 0.20 against a measured 0.200. On the number itself, calibration worked.

The interval is the more interesting result, because it does not narrow enough. The relative half-width goes from 1.76 to 1.13, and the guardrail keeps holding.

Figure 3 shows what did move. The reference and control curves read a return between 1.5 and 3 across every spend level the optimizer might consider. The calibrated curve reads about 0.2 across the same range and passes through the experimental point. The experiment did not nudge one number; it relocated the curve.

So why is the interval still wide? Not because the experiment was imprecise. Its standard error was 0.075 on a point estimate of 0.200, which is a tight measurement. The width comes from the translation, and almost all of it from one term: the eight test markets ran at about a fifth of the channel’s daily spend rate, and that gap alone multiplies the standard error by roughly two. What is left in the interval is not doubt about what happened in those markets. It is doubt about whether the rest of the country behaves like them.

That distinction is worth more than a released guardrail would have been, because it names the next experiment. A longer test would not help much; Search’s carryover is already fully captured in eight weeks. A test in more markets would.

The allocation, meanwhile, is the anticlimax, and that is the finding.

Search sits at the floor in every fit. OOH and Meta take the released budget in every fit. What moved between the control and the calibrated model is TV/CTV and TikTok by a few tenths of a point, well inside the noise of a planning conversation. Calibration changed what the model knows, not where it points.

Section 6.3 left one question for this chapter: not whether the last 30% of Search pays for itself, which it doesn’t, but whether those dollars earn more somewhere else. The calibrated model answers it. Search’s marginal return tops out at 0.54, and every other channel’s interval starts above 0.95. The guardrail’s width rule still reads HOLD, because a relative half-width of 1.13 on a return of 0.22 is wide in ratio terms even though it is narrow in dollars. The guardrail was written for a model acting alone. This decision doesn’t rest on the model alone: the experiment’s hurdle settled the cut, and the portfolio comparison settles where the money goes.

That is worth stating plainly, because it is easy to sell calibration as the thing that finds the answer. It isn’t. Section 6.3’s hurdle rule had already decided this quarter’s cut: the experiment’s 95% interval topped out at 0.361 against a break-even ROAS of 2.50, and no reasonable reading of that supports keeping the spend. What calibration bought is a model that carries the evidence forward into every allocation after this one, a marginal estimate that is now right rather than eight times too high, and a precise statement of what the next test has to be.

Run the same calibration at Section 6.3’s conservative width, the standard error clustered on the eight matched pairs rather than the sixteen markets, and the marginal estimate barely moves, to 0.23, while the relative half-width goes to 1.46. The verdict does not change. Neither does the allocation. A recommendation that survives both defensible widths is one you can take to a meeting; one that flipped between them would have told you the evidence had not settled it.

The point of calibration is not to make the MMM agree with the experiment. The point is to see whether better causal evidence changes the decision.

Before Acting on the Calibrated Plan

Four questions, in the order a review meeting should ask them.

  1. Estimand alignment. Does the experiment measure the same outcome, the same contrast, and an operating point the recommendation will actually visit?
  2. Experiment credibility. Was it powered for the decision, delivered as designed, free of spillover and calendar shocks?
  3. Model integrity. After the refit, are fit, response curves, and the other channels still plausible?
  4. Decision stability. Across defensible translation widths, does the recommended action hold? If it reverses between them, the evidence hasn’t resolved the decision, whatever the point estimate says.

Data quality and general model validation belong to Section 6.2 and don’t need repeating here.

Calibration as a Learning Loop

You don’t need an experiment for every channel. Test where spend is material, where the model’s interval is wide, and where a result could change the action. Record the model’s prediction before the test, as Section 6.2’s decision card did, so the program measures its own calibration. Then refresh the model before the next allocation, and let the next disagreement choose the next test.

Key Takeaways

  • Match the estimand, then translate rather than transplant. A marginal measurement belongs on a marginal parameter, corrected for carryover and widened for scale, with the experiment’s uncertainty traveling with it.
  • Refit the whole system, not just the calibrated channel. Calibration moved Search’s marginal return from eight times too high to nearly exact.
  • Judge by whether the decision changes. The interval never narrowed enough to release the guardrail and the allocation never moved, which is the finding and which names the next test.
