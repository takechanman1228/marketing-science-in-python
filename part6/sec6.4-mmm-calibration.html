6.4 Calibrating MMM with Experiments

We now have two results for Google Search. The MMM from Section 6.2 covers all five channels, but its Search estimate is very uncertain. The experiment from Section 6.3 measured Search precisely, but it measured only Search.

The next decision needs both. Cutting Search frees up budget, and deciding where to move it means comparing all five channels, which only the MMM can do. So we want the MMM to use what the experiment learned about Search.

What Is Calibration?

Calibration is the process of using experiment results to improve an MMM. In a Bayesian MMM it works through the prior. The experiment’s estimate for a channel becomes that channel’s prior, and the model is fit again (Figure 1). The refit model combines the two sources: for the tested channel it relies mainly on the experiment, and for everything else it relies on the historical data.

Our case shows why this is needed. Before the test, the notebook asked the Section 6.2 model to predict the experiment’s result: the iROAS of a 30% Search cut over eight weeks. The model predicted 2.95, with a 90% interval from 0.34 to 8.48. The experiment measured 1.50.

The model’s estimate was about twice the measured value, and its interval was too wide to support a decision. Search spend had barely moved in three years, so the model had little information about Search beyond its prior. Calibration gives it that information.

Calibration takes three steps, and the rest of this section follows them:

  1. Identify which quantity in the model the experiment measured.
  2. Convert the experiment’s result into a prior for that quantity.
  3. Refit the model and compare it with the uncalibrated one.

Step 1: Average, Incremental, and Marginal ROAS

A prior in Meridian is placed on a specific quantity: either a channel’s average ROAS or its marginal ROAS. The experiment’s result has to go on the quantity it actually measured. So the first step is to distinguish three kinds of ROAS.

Suppose a market spends $100 a week on Search. All three kinds are a change in revenue divided by a change in spend. They differ in the range of spend they cover (Figure 2).

  • Average ROAS covers $0 to $100: all of today’s spend. The MMM reports it.
  • Incremental ROAS of the 30% cut covers $70 to $100: the spend the test removed. The experiment measured it.
  • Marginal ROAS covers $100 to $101: the next dollar. The MMM reports it, and the optimizer allocates on it.

On a saturating response curve, the first dollars earn more than the last ones. Average ROAS is therefore the highest of the three. The iROAS of the cut and marginal ROAS both describe the top of the curve, so they are close to each other and lower than the average.

The experiment measured the iROAS of a 30% cut. That is closest to marginal ROAS, so the result should inform the prior on marginal ROAS.

What happens with the wrong choice. Meridian’s default prior, roi_m, is on average ROAS. If the experiment’s result is placed there, the model treats a number measured on the last 30% of spend as the return on all spend. The notebook fits this version for comparison. It estimates Search’s average ROAS at 1.96, while the true value in the data is 3.30, about 40% too low. An average-ROAS prior is the right choice only for an experiment that turns a channel off completely.

Step 2: Converting the Experiment into a Prior

Meridian places the prior on marginal ROAS when media_prior_type="mroi" is set. The experiment’s iROAS is close to marginal ROAS, but the two are not identical. The experiment covered a 30% cut, in eight markets, over eight weeks. Marginal ROAS describes the next dollar, for the whole country, including sales that arrive later. Each of these differences needs an adjustment (Table 1).

Duration raises the center of the prior. A cut made in week eight still reduces revenue in weeks nine and ten, after measurement has stopped. With Search’s one-week half-life, about 12% of the effect falls outside the window. For a slow channel such as TV/CTV, an eight-week window would miss about half.

Spend scale and translation uncertainty widen the prior. The test covered about a fifth of national Search spend, so its result is less certain when applied to the whole channel. Translation uncertainty adds a minimum width of 15% of the center, because a 30% cut is only an approximation of the next dollar.

After the three adjustments, the experiment’s 1.50 (standard error 0.257) becomes a prior on Search’s marginal ROAS with a mean of 1.71 and a standard deviation of 0.69. The prior is deliberately wider than the experiment. The experiment is precise about the eight markets it tested and less precise about national marginal ROAS, and the prior describes the second.

Duration and spend scale follow the adjustments Meridian documents for turning an experiment into a prior. Translation uncertainty is this book’s addition, because Meridian’s procedure assumes an experiment against zero spend and ours was a partial cut.

# Imports as in Section 6.2. Both helpers are defined in notebook Step 15.
center, se = calibrated_prior_moments(
    estimate=1.500, standard_error=0.257,
    capture_proportion=0.8756,       # duration: share of the effect inside 8 weeks
    # taus: duration, spend scale, recency (zero, because the test is recent)
    taus=[0.142, 3.606, 0.000],
    translation_share=0.15,          # translation uncertainty
)                                    # -> center 1.71, SE 0.69
search_mu, search_sigma = lognormal_from_mean_std(center, se)

# mroi_mu, mroi_sigma: one entry per channel. Search gets search_mu and
# search_sigma; the other four keep a weak prior (notebook Step 15).
calibrated_prior = prior_distribution.PriorDistribution(
    mroi_m=tfp.distributions.LogNormal(
        loc=tf.constant(mroi_mu, dtype=tf.float32),
        scale=tf.constant(mroi_sigma, dtype=tf.float32),
        name=constants.MROI_M,
    ),
    # adstock and saturation priors from Section 6.2
    alpha_m=prior.alpha_m, ec_m=prior.ec_m,
)

# Same lag and holdout weeks as Section 6.2, so the only change is the prior.
calibrated_spec = spec.ModelSpec(
    prior=calibrated_prior, media_prior_type="mroi", max_lag=12,
    adstock_decay_spec="geometric", holdout_id=holdout_id,
)

The other four channels keep their weak priors. The model is then fit again on the same data with the same settings.

Step 3: The Model Before and After Calibration

To see what the experiment changed, we compare two fits that differ only in the Search prior. Both use marginal-ROAS priors. The first has weak priors on every channel, and the second has the experiment-based prior on Search (Table 2). Section 6.2’s model is not the baseline here, because it used average-ROAS priors and the comparison would mix two changes.

The estimate barely changed. Marginal ROAS is 1.95 without the experiment and 2.00 with it. The uncertainty changed a lot: the 90% interval was [0.18, 7.03] and is now [0.93, 3.63], less than half as wide.

This is what makes the model usable for Search. In Section 6.2 the Search recommendation was put on hold because its interval was too wide. The calibrated interval passes that rule.

The budget recommendation for Search is the same in both fits: a 30% cut, the largest the optimizer was allowed (Figure 3). Calibration left the direction unchanged and made the model precise enough to support it.

For the other channels, the calibrated model recommends a 30% increase for OOH and Meta, a 16.4% cut for TV/CTV, and an 11.4% cut for TikTok. The TV/CTV and TikTok numbers differ from Section 6.2, which had cuts of 30.0% and 0.2%. Figure 3 shows where that change comes from. It already appears in the fit without the experiment, so it is caused by the switch to marginal-ROAS priors.

Using the Calibrated Plan

The calibrated plan contains two kinds of recommendation, and they are handled differently.

The Search cut has been tested, so it can go ahead. Roll it out in stages. Extend the cut to more markets, keep a randomly chosen group at the old budget as a control, and confirm that the iROAS stays below 2.50.

The model recommends putting the budget freed up by the Search cut into 30% increases for OOH and Meta. Raise one of them by 30% in randomized matched pairs, record the model’s predicted iROAS before launch, and calibrate again with the result. In general, test a channel when its spend is large, the model’s interval is wide, and the result could change the plan.

The platform dashboard answers a narrower question. The Search dashboard reports a ROAS based on attribution: it counts every order that follows a click on a Search ad, including orders that would have happened without the ad. Its number is therefore usually higher than the experimental iROAS. Use the dashboard to compare keywords and creatives within Search, and use the experiment and the MMM to set the Search budget.

This is the workflow for Part 6 as a whole. The MMM proposes a budget across all channels. An experiment tests the recommendation that is both uncertain and costly. Calibration brings the experimental result back into the model, and the cycle repeats with the next test.

Key Takeaways

  • Calibration uses an experiment’s result as a prior in the MMM, so the model relies on the experiment for the tested channel and on historical data for the rest.
  • Put the result on the quantity the experiment measured. A partial cut is closest to marginal ROAS; placed on average ROAS, it made the estimate about 40% too low here.
  • Make the prior wider than the experiment, to cover later weeks, untested markets, and the gap between a partial cut and the next dollar. Calibration then narrowed Search’s interval far more than it moved the estimate.
