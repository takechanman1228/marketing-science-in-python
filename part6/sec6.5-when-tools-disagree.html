6.5 Making Decisions When Measurement Tools Disagree

Three Numbers, One Decision

Return to the CFO’s question from Section 6.1, a year and a full measurement stack later. Attribution reports a ROAS of 4.2 for retargeting and almost nothing for TV. The MMM credits TV/CTV with a meaningful share of conversions and puts retargeting’s average ROAS at 1.8. A conversion-lift test on retargeting comes back at an incremental ROAS of 0.9. The CFO wants one number. Which is it?

Not until the decision is named. If the question is whether the last dollars of retargeting create incremental value, the experiment’s 0.9 is the place to start, provided the test passes its credibility checks. The three tools were built to answer different questions, on different data, over different windows, so they will disagree, and a stack whose tools always agreed would be redundant. But disagreement isn’t automatically harmless either. Sometimes it means the tools are measuring different things. Sometimes it means one of them has failed: tracking that misses half the exposures, an underpowered test, a model that can’t separate two channels that always move together. The job is not to make the numbers agree. It’s to decide which evidence is relevant and credible for the decision in front of you, act on it, and use the gap to plan the next test.

Start with the Decision, Not the Metric

The fastest way out of a three-number argument is to name the decision. Each kind of decision has a primary source of evidence.

Decision Primary evidence Credibility check
What is the causal effect of this specific spend change? Experiment Estimand, power, implementation, spillover
How should the total budget be split across channels? Calibrated MMM Identification, response curves, decision stability
Which keyword, audience, bid, or creative should change? Attribution and platform data Tracking coverage, selection on intent

“Primary” means where to start. It is a default, not a hierarchy, and the third column is the price of using it. Part 6’s central question, where the next media dollar should go, is the second row: that is why Section 6.2 built the MMM, Section 6.3 tested the one channel whose estimate was too wide to act on, and Section 6.4 fed the result back. The CFO’s retargeting question is the first row. Attribution’s 4.2 was never a candidate for it.

Diagnose the Gap

When two tools disagree and the decision needs both, work through three questions in order.

Are they measuring the same thing? Attribution distributes observed conversions across touchpoints; a conversion that would have happened anyway still gets credited. An MMM estimates each channel’s average contribution over the modeled history. An experiment estimates the incremental effect of one specific spend change. Attribution’s 4.2 and the test’s 0.9 are not two estimates of one quantity. One allocates observed conversions; the other estimates a causal contrast. Average and marginal ROAS differ by construction too, as Section 6.4 showed. In the Section 6.2 data, TV/CTV’s true average return is 4.17 and its true marginal return 1.80, from the same curve: one measured from zero spend, the other at the operating point. Neither is wrong. They answer different questions.

Are they measuring the same scope? Time window, audience, geography, conversion definition, operating point. The largest scope gap is usually time. Attribution sees a click window; an MMM sees carryover through adstock; a test sees only its own weeks. In the Section 6.2 data, TV/CTV’s true marginal return is 1.80, but a 30% pull-back measured over eight weeks would read 0.83. Two things pull it down. About a fifth of TV’s carryover lands after the window closes, so the test never sees it. The rest is the contrast: a 30% cut averages the response curve across a whole block of spend, and on a curve that bends, that average sits below the slope at today’s level. Neither is a failed test. Both are the estimand the test was built to measure, read as though it were a different one.

Is each piece of evidence credible enough for this decision? For attribution: is tracking coverage adequate, and could selection on intent explain the number? For the MMM: is the channel identified, or does its interval span an order of magnitude? Search’s headline ROAS of 3.53 in Section 6.2 came with an interval from 0.4 to 10, and Section 6.4 showed that the number was mostly the prior. A number like that is not evidence for or against anything. For the experiment: was it powered for the decision, implemented as designed, and free of spillover?

Three short cases cover most of what shows up in practice.

Attribution says cut, the MMM says keep. Upper-funnel channels create demand that surfaces later as branded search or direct traffic. Attribution misses most of it; the MMM can capture it as aggregate lift, provided the channel is identified. For cross-channel allocation, start from the calibrated MMM, and if the channel is material, schedule a geo test with a window long enough for its carryover.

Attribution says strong, the test says weak. Retargeting is the canonical case. It targets people who already visited, so the tracked paths are full of conversions the ads didn’t cause. For the incremental-budget question the experiment owns it; check its power and scope, then act.

The MMM says strong, the test says weak. Both claim to measure incremental contribution, so this one needs the first two questions. Check the horizon against the channel’s adstock, and check the operating point: the model describes the historical spend range, the test perturbed one point. If the estimands line up and the test is credible, calibrate as in Section 6.4, carrying the test’s uncertainty rather than shrinking it. If they don’t line up, the honest move is a wider model interval or no update, plus a better-designed test. Not a prior widened until the model stops complaining.

Decide, Then Learn

The protocol is short enough to agree on before the numbers arrive, which is when it has to be agreed on. Name the decision. Name the estimand that decision needs. Pick the best-aligned evidence that passes its credibility check. Act with the uncertainty attached and with guardrails on the size of the move, as the interval-width rule did in Section 6.2. Then treat the gap between the tools as information about where to test next; the next experiment is the disagreement that is material, would change an action, and can actually be reduced by a test, the same three conditions Section 6.4 used to choose this one.

Run the meetings against deadlines, not against convergence. Bring intervals rather than point estimates, decide on schedule, and record the prediction before each test so the stack measures its own calibration over time. In Part 6 that loop closed once: the model recommended a cut it couldn’t defend, the test settled it, and the calibrated model carried the evidence into the next allocation. Measurement maturity isn’t a stack whose numbers agree. It’s knowing which evidence is good enough to make the next decision, and what to run so that the one after it is easier. Key Takeaways

  • Attribution, MMM, and experiments disagree because they measure different quantities, at different scopes, from different data. Diagnose the estimand, the scope, and the credibility before you pick one.
  • Start from the decision and use the best-aligned evidence, then decide on schedule with intervals and guardrails. Run the meetings against deadlines, not convergence, and let the material disagreement choose the next test.
