6.4 When Attribution, MMM, and Experiments Disagree

Return to the CFO question from Section 6.1, one year and a full measurement stack later. Attribution reports a ROAS of 4.2 for retargeting and almost nothing for TV. The MMM credits TV/CTV with a meaningful share of conversion volume and estimates retargeting’s average ROAS at 1.8. A geo-lift test on retargeting comes back at an incremental ROAS of 0.9.

It is common to wonder which tool is wrong when the numbers do not match. In practice, attribution, MMM, and experiments are designed to answer different questions, using different data and time frames. Disagreement is normal and signals that each tool is doing its job.

The bigger risk is not disagreement, but inaction. The following sections explain why these conflicts happen, outline the three most common types of disagreement, and provide practical steps for making decisions even when the tools do not agree.

Why the Numbers Differ by Construction

Most conflicts trace back to four structural differences between the tools.

Source of disagreement What it means in practice
Time windows Attribution evaluates short click and view windows. MMM, through adstock, captures carryover that plays out over weeks; Section 6.2 modeled TV with a 6-week half-life. A four-week test measures only what happens inside the test.
Data inputs Attribution sees user-level tracked paths, and misses whatever tracking misses. MMM sees aggregate spend and revenue. Experiments see the difference between test and control geos.
Estimand Attribution distributes observed conversions across touchpoints. MMM estimates each channel’s average contribution by regression. Experiments estimate the incremental effect of one specific spend change. These are three different definitions of “effectiveness.”
External factors MMM explicitly controls for seasonality, promotions, and macro drivers. Attribution does not. Experiments difference them out through the control group.

Instead of asking which number is right, ask which number is built for the decision in front of you. The rest of this chapter focuses on the three types of conflicts you are most likely to encounter in practice.

Attribution Says Cut, MMM Says Keep

This is the most common conflict. Attribution concentrates credit on lower-funnel channels that sit close to the conversion event: paid search, retargeting, affiliates. Upper-funnel channels create demand that surfaces later as branded search or direct traffic, so their contribution is invisible in path data while remaining visible to the MMM as aggregate lift.

The standard scenario: attribution suggests cutting upper-funnel spend that “never shows up in converting paths,” while the MMM attributes a meaningful share of conversion volume to those same channels.

How to act: Match the tool to the decision. Use the calibrated MMM for cross-channel budget allocation and to set channel-level guardrails. Use attribution for within-channel decisions like creative testing, keyword bidding, or audience selection. In meetings, clarify whether you are making a budget decision or an execution decision.

The Test Contradicts Attribution

Experiments are the ground truth for incrementality, and they can break against attribution in either direction.

The test shows less than attribution claims. Attribution counts users who would have converted anyway. Retargeting is the canonical case: it targets people who already visited the site, so the tracked paths are full of conversions that the ads did not cause.

The test shows more than attribution claims. Channels with weak tracking coverage (CTV, streaming audio, much of YouTube) are underrepresented or absent in path data. Attribution reports near-zero contribution because it literally cannot see the exposures, while a geo holdout reveals meaningful incremental lift.

How to act: Use experiments to make go/no-go decisions about whether a channel deserves investment. Continue to use attribution for tactical decisions in channels where tracking is reliable. If there is a large gap between tested and attributed ROAS, treat it as a signal about where your tracking is incomplete, not as a sign that one tool is broken.

The Test Contradicts the MMM

This is the subtler conflict, because both tools claim to measure incremental contribution. The typical shape: the MMM, fit on two to three years of weekly data, shows a channel as effective; a four-to-eight-week geo test on the same channel shows little or no lift.

Two mechanisms explain most of these gaps. The first is time horizons. Through adstock, the MMM picks up carryover and slow-building brand effects that a short test window structurally cannot observe. The test is not wrong; it is answering a shorter-run question. The second is operating points. The MMM’s coefficients describe average behavior over the historical spend range, while the test perturbs spend at one specific point on the saturation curve.

How to act: Use the experimental result to calibrate the MMM, as described in Section 6.3. Focus on whether the estimates are directionally consistent, rather than expecting them to match exactly.

A Decision Protocol

The three conflicts resolve the same way: identify which tool owns the decision at stake, act on that tool’s number, and treat the disagreement itself as information about where to test next. The table below summarizes the playbook.

Situation Trust most Decision at stake Action
Attribution says cut, the MMM says keep The calibrated MMM, plus a follow-up test Upper-funnel budget Do not cut outright; schedule a geo test on the disputed channel
Attribution says strong, the test says weak The experiment Channel incrementality Cap or scale back spend; retargeting is the classic case
The MMM says strong, the test says weak Both, allowing for the horizon gap Calibration Widen the experimental prior; check the test window against the channel’s adstock
All three are uncertain No single tool Risk management Hold the budget steady and design the experiment that narrows the widest interval

A protocol only works if it is agreed on before the numbers arrive. Fix the decision rules in advance: who owns which decision, and what evidence is good enough to act on. Then run the meetings against deadlines, not against convergence: bring ranges rather than point estimates, make the call on schedule, and feed the gap between prediction and outcome into the next round of experiments.

Key Takeaways

  • Attribution, MMM, and experiments measure different quantities, over different windows, from different data. Disagreement is structural, and a stack whose tools always agreed would be redundant.
  • Route conflicts by decision type: the calibrated MMM owns cross-channel budget guardrails, experiments own channel go/no-go, and attribution owns in-channel tactics.
  • When a test undercuts attribution, suspect selection on intent (retargeting is the canonical case). When a test exceeds attribution, suspect tracking gaps (CTV, streaming). Both gaps are information about your data, not errors in your tools.
  • When a test contradicts the MMM, check the time horizons and the operating point on the saturation curve, then calibrate rather than replace (Section 6.3).
  • Fix the decision rules before the numbers arrive: who owns which decision, and what is good enough to act on. Bring ranges to the meeting, decide on schedule rather than waiting for the numbers to converge, and feed the gap between prediction and outcome into the next experiment.
